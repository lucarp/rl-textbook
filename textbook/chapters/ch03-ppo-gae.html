Chapter 3 — Trust regions, PPO, and advantage estimation

Chapter 2 left you holding an estimator: push up the log-probability of actions that turned out better than expected, push down the ones that turned out worse, and weight each push by the advantage \(\hat{A}_t\). That is a direction, not a step size. Nothing in Chapter 2 told you how far to move before the estimate you built stops being trustworthy. That question separates a policy gradient that works from one that destroys itself in an afternoon.

You have already run PPO — on your differential-drive rover, through Stable-Baselines3, with a config you probably copied and tweaked. By the end of this chapter you should understand what every one of those knobs was doing, and why the TensorBoard curves looked the way they did.

One thing up front. PPO will not survive the jump to the policies of Part III: flow-matching vision-language-action policies, or VLAs — policies built on a pretrained vision-language model that output robot actions directly (Chapter 8), trained by flow matching, a generative recipe that turns noise into an action along a learned velocity field. Chapter 7 gives the technical reason PPO cannot be used on them; Chapter 9 makes the comparison explicit. The advantage machinery in Section 3.5, however, carries all the way to the LeHome capstone in Chapter 15. If you can only deeply learn one half of this chapter, learn the second half.

3.1 Why step size is the whole game

3.1.1 Supervised learning forgives; reinforcement learning does not

Think about a supervised training run — an image classifier. You take a gradient step that is far too large and the loss jumps. The next minibatch comes from the same fixed dataset, its gradient points back toward the good region, and within a few dozen steps you are roughly where you were. The dataset does not care what your model did.

On-policy reinforcement learning has no such object. The data is the policy. Every state in your next batch was reached because the current policy chose the actions that led there. A destructive update produces a worse policy, which collects worse data, which produces gradients computed on states the good policy would never have visited. The distribution of states you train on moves with every update, and it moves because of your updates.

An analogy. In a self-hosting compiler — one written in the language it compiles — you use version N of the binary to build version N+1. Introduce a subtle code-generation bug and N+1 is slightly wrong. N+1 then builds an N+2 that inherits the bug and adds its own. The corruption compounds because the tool is its own input, exactly as the policy is both the thing being improved and the source of its own training data.

Where the analogy breaks down. With a compiler you have a known-good binary in a bucket, and restoring it costs only the time since the bad commit. Restoring a policy checkpoint costs every environment step collected since — hours in simulation, days on real hardware. And the corruption is statistical, not logical: no diff, no stack trace, often no single step you can blame.

3.1.2 What a collapse actually looks like

The failure mode is stereotyped, so you can recognize it from the plots. In a healthy run ep_rew_mean climbs raggedly; entropy — a measure of how spread out the policy's action distribution still is, unpacked in Section 3.4 — decays gently; and the approximate KL divergence, a running measure of how much an update changed the policy's behavior (made precise in Section 3.3), sits in a narrow band. A collapse announces itself in four curves over two or three rollouts. The approximate KL spikes by an order of magnitude. Entropy either falls off a cliff toward zero or blows upward as the Gaussian's standard deviation diverges. The value loss — the critic's squared prediction error, Section 3.4 — explodes, because the critic's targets moved out from under it. And ep_rew_mean drops to the floor and stays there. The KL spike usually comes first, by a rollout or two. That is why you monitor it: it is the earliest warning that an update is moving further than the data supports.

3.1.3 Why it is often unrecoverable

Why does the reward curve stay flat afterward instead of re-climbing? Suppose your rover had learned to approach the goal region and was collecting the occasional terminal reward. Those near-goal states were in its data, so the critic had learned something about them and the gradient had signal there. After the collapse the rover spins near the spawn point and never reaches the goal again. Those informative states are absent from every later batch.

So the gradient says nothing about them, the critic's estimates for them decay toward the network's default output, and the policy must rediscover the approach behavior by random exploration — often from a policy whose entropy has already collapsed, so it cannot explore.

This is why step size is not a detail you get to postpone. In on-policy RL, controlling how far each update moves the policy is the algorithm. The next three sections are three strategies for one goal: extract as much improvement as possible from a batch without moving so far that the batch stops describing the policy.

3.2 Reusing a batch: importance sampling from scratch

3.2.1 What we want, and the obvious thing to try

Collecting data is expensive. On the rover in simulation a 16,384-step batch takes seconds; on the SO-101 arm it takes minutes of real robot time and human supervision. The vanilla policy gradient from Chapter 2 is wasteful with it: the estimator is valid only at the exact parameters \(\theta_{old}\) that collected the data, so you get one gradient step per batch and then throw the batch away.

The obvious thing to try: do ten gradient steps on the same batch. Why not? The data is right there.

Here is exactly how that fails. After the first step your parameters are no longer \(\theta_{old}\); call them \(\theta_1\). The policy gradient theorem says the gradient of \(J\) is an expectation over actions drawn from the current policy, and your stored actions were drawn from \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\), not \(\pi_{\theta_1}\). So the second step averages over the wrong distribution — not merely noisy but systematically wrong, and more wrong with each step.

Put that on the rover. The policy is a diagonal Gaussian over wheel commands. At one stored state its mean left-wheel command was \(0.30\) with standard deviation \(0.10\), and the action stored there was \(0.36\) — an unremarkable sample, six tenths of a standard deviation out. Ten unconstrained gradient steps later, the mean for that state has drifted to \(0.16\), so the stored action now sits two standard deviations into the tail: something the current policy would produce about once in forty tries. The gradient does not know that. It keeps pushing on the sample at full weight. Set n_epochs=10 on rl-rover — ten passes over each stored batch — with clipping turned off, and you can watch the consequence: reward climbs for two or three rollouts, then falls to the floor and stays there.

3.2.2 The fix: reweight by how much more likely the new policy is

The repair is importance sampling — estimating an average under one distribution using samples drawn from a different one, by reweighting each sample by the ratio of the two probabilities. Define the importance ratio:

\[ \rho_t(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)}, \]

where \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\) is the frozen snapshot of the policy that collected the batch, and \(\pi_\theta\) is the policy as it stands right now, partway through the several passes we are about to take over that batch.

Put numbers on it. Suppose at state \(s_t\) the old policy assigned the action actually taken a probability of \(0.20\), and the current policy assigns it \(0.30\). Then \(\rho_t = 0.30 / 0.20 = 1.5\): the new policy is one and a half times as likely to have produced this sample, so it counts one and a half times as much in the average. Had the new policy dropped to \(0.10\), then \(\rho_t = 0.10/0.20 = 0.5\) and it counts half as much. If the policy has not moved, \(\rho_t = 1\) and nothing changes.

The reason this is exactly right, not merely plausible, is one line of algebra. For any function \(f\) of the action,

\[ \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}}\!\left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(a)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a)} f(a) \right] \;=\; \sum_a \pi_{\theta_{old}}(a)\, \frac{\pi_\theta(a)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a)}\, f(a) \;=\; \sum_a \pi_\theta(a)\, f(a) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta}\!\left[ f(a) \right]. \]

(That display is written with sums, for a discrete action set. For the continuous actions your rover takes, replace \(\sum_a\) with \(\int da\) and read each \(\pi\) as a density rather than a probability; the cancellation is character-for-character identical.)

The \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\) cancels. Samples from the old policy, weighted by the ratio, give an unbiased average under the new policy. Note what this requires: the actual probability density of the action. In code, \(\rho_t = \exp(\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) - \log \pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_t \mid s_t))\), in log-space for numerical stability. For a Gaussian policy that log-probability is a closed form you can differentiate. Remember the requirement; Chapter 7 is where it stops being satisfiable.

3.2.3 The surrogate objective and the lie it tells

Plug the ratio into the objective. The result is a surrogate objective — a stand-in we optimize because the thing we actually care about is not directly computable from the data we have:

\[ L(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\, \rho_t(\theta)\, \hat{A}_t \,\right] \]
In plain words
The left side, \(L(\theta)\), is the score we push up by changing the policy parameters \(\theta\). The \(\mathbb{E}_t\) means "average over all timesteps in the batch you collected". For one timestep: \(\hat{A}_t\) estimates how much better than average the action taken at time \(t\) turned out to be — positive means better than the policy's own expectation, negative worse. \(\rho_t(\theta)\) is how many times more likely the current policy is to take that same action than the old policy was. Multiplying them says: to raise the score, become more likely to take actions with positive \(\hat{A}_t\) and less likely to take actions with negative \(\hat{A}_t\).

This surrogate has a useful property: at \(\theta = \theta_{old}\), where every \(\rho_t = 1\), its gradient equals the true policy gradient of \(J(\theta)\). Here is why, because it is worth seeing rather than believing. Differentiate the ratio, remembering that \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)\) is a stored number, not a function of \(\theta\): \(\nabla_\theta \rho_t = \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) / \pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)\). Evaluate that at \(\theta = \theta_{old}\), where the denominator is the numerator's own probability, and apply the identity \(\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta = \pi_\theta \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\) from Chapter 2. The \(\pi_\theta\) cancels, leaving \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\), so the surrogate's gradient is \(\mathbb{E}_t[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \hat{A}_t]\) — the score-function estimator you built in Chapter 2. PPO's first gradient step on a fresh batch and REINFORCE-with-a-baseline's step are the same step. Everything PPO adds is about the steps after it.

Ascending the surrogate therefore starts out exactly correct. But it is only a local approximation, and you should know precisely which part is a lie. The ratio corrects for the new policy choosing different actions at the states in your batch. It does nothing about the new policy visiting different states. The surrogate quietly assumes the state distribution is frozen at whatever \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\) produced.

Think of an A/B test on a web service. You ran variant B on 20% of traffic and now want to estimate what happens at 30%. Reweighting your logged sessions is reasonable. But if variant B also changes which users come back tomorrow, your reweighted logs describe a population that no longer exists. Where the analogy breaks down: in an A/B test that drift is slow and you can re-measure. In RL it happens inside one training iteration, and the next batch is already coming from the drifted policy.

So the further \(\rho_t\) strays from 1, the bigger the lie. Sections 3.3 and 3.4 are two mechanisms for ascending \(L(\theta)\) while keeping \(\rho_t\) near 1.

3.3 TRPO in one section

3.3.1 The constrained problem

Trust Region Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2015) takes the honest route. If the surrogate is only trustworthy near \(\theta_{old}\), say so explicitly: maximize \(L(\theta)\) subject to a hard constraint that the average KL divergence between \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\) and \(\pi_\theta\), taken over the states in the batch, stays below a small threshold such as \(0.01\).

The region of parameter space where that constraint holds is the trust region: the neighborhood in which you trust your approximation. Inside it, improving the surrogate provably improves a lower bound on the true objective \(J(\theta)\). That is a real guarantee, and more than PPO can claim.

3.3.2 KL divergence in plain words

KL divergence measures how different two distributions are, and the definition \(D_{KL}(p \,\|\, q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[\log p(x) - \log q(x)]\) is not illuminating on its own. Bind the symbols first: \(p\) is the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\), \(q\) is the new policy \(\pi_\theta\), and \(\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\) says the average is taken over actions drawn the way the old policy would draw them. The useful intuition: how surprised is the old policy by the new one? If the two agree everywhere, every log-difference is zero and the KL is zero. If the new policy has taken mass away from actions the old one considered likely, those are precisely the actions you keep drawing; each one has \(\log p\) large and \(\log q\) small, so the log-differences are large and positive and the KL grows. Moving mass onto actions the old policy never took barely registers directly — you almost never draw those actions, so they almost never enter the average. It registers because that mass had to come from somewhere.

It is not symmetric — \(D_{KL}(p\|q) \ne D_{KL}(q\|p)\) in general — and it is not a distance. For our purposes it is a scalar that reads zero when the policy has not moved and grows as it does, in units that reflect the shape of the distribution rather than the size of the parameter change. That distinction matters: two policies can have very different parameters and behave identically. KL measures behavior, not parameters.

3.3.3 Why almost nobody runs TRPO

The implementation is where it falls apart. Enforcing the constraint exactly requires a second-order object: the Fisher information matrix, which is the curvature of the KL at \(\theta_{old}\) — a statement of how fast behavior changes per unit of parameter change. For a network with millions of parameters you cannot even store that matrix. So TRPO never forms it. It computes only the matrix's product with one vector at a time (a Fisher-vector product, obtainable from a pair of backward passes), solves for the step direction with conjugate gradient — an iterative linear solver that needs nothing but such products — and then backtracks, halving the proposed step repeatedly, until the KL constraint really holds.

That is three pieces of nontrivial numerical machinery around every update. It is slow, fiddly to get right, and interacts badly with architectures that share a trunk between policy and value function. TRPO's lasting contribution is the framing: a policy update as constrained optimization over a trust region. The practical tool arrived two years later with none of the machinery.

3.4 PPO: clip the surrogate

3.4.1 The clipped objective

Proximal Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2017) asks a different question: instead of forbidding large steps, what if we stop paying for them? If moving \(\rho_t\) beyond a small interval around 1 earns no extra objective value, plain gradient ascent has no reason to go there.

\[ L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\, \min\!\Big( \rho_t(\theta)\, \hat{A}_t,\;\; \mathrm{clip}\big(\rho_t(\theta),\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon\big)\, \hat{A}_t \Big) \right] \]
In plain words
For each timestep in the batch, compute two numbers. The first is the plain surrogate term \(\rho_t \hat{A}_t\) from Section 3.2, where \(\hat{A}_t\) is the advantage estimate and \(\rho_t\) the importance ratio. The second is the same product, but with the ratio first squashed into the window \([1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon\,]\): \(\mathrm{clip}\) returns \(1-\epsilon\) if \(\rho_t\) is below it, \(1+\epsilon\) if above, \(\rho_t\) unchanged in between. The clip parameter \(\epsilon\), typically 0.1 to 0.3, sets the window's width. Then take \(\min\) of the two — whichever is worse for us — and average over the batch. Preferring the smaller of an honest estimate and a capped one is what makes the objective refuse to reward large moves.

The \(\min\) wrapped around the \(\mathrm{clip}\) is the part everyone finds confusing at first, and the confusion is legitimate: two nested operations that look like they do the same job. They do not. Unpack it one sign of the advantage at a time, following Figure 3.1 as you go.

3.4.2 Case one: the advantage was positive

\(\hat{A}_t \gt 0\) means the action turned out better than the policy expected, so we want to raise its probability — we want \(\rho_t\) above 1. As it grows, the unclipped term \(\rho_t \hat{A}_t\) grows linearly, and the clipped term grows with it until \(\rho_t\) hits \(1+\epsilon\), where it freezes at \((1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\). Past that point the clipped term is the smaller of the two, the \(\min\) selects it, and the objective is flat.

Flat means zero gradient. Once the current policy assigns this action \(1+\epsilon\) times its old probability, this sample stops pushing at all. With \(\epsilon = 0.2\): raise it by 20%, then stop. The sample has said what it has to say.

3.4.3 Case two: the advantage was negative

\(\hat{A}_t \lt 0\) means the action was worse than expected, so we want \(\rho_t\) below 1. Because \(\hat{A}_t\) is negative, shrinking \(\rho_t\) makes \(\rho_t \hat{A}_t\) less negative, which improves the objective. That improvement stops at \(\rho_t = 1-\epsilon\), where the clipped term freezes at \((1-\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\); below that the clipped term is the more negative of the two, the \(\min\) selects it, and the gradient dies again.

Now the interesting half. What if \(\rho_t\) goes up instead — if an earlier minibatch update in this same epoch accidentally raised the probability of an action we know was bad? Then \(\rho_t \hat{A}_t\) grows more and more negative with no cap, and the \(\min\) selects that unclipped branch. The mistake is punished at full strength, with no ceiling.

3.4.4 The asymmetry is the entire point

Look at Figure 3.1 as a pair. The incentive to improve is capped; the penalty for getting worse is not. That asymmetry is what the \(\min\) buys, and it is why the objective is called pessimistic. If you simply clipped \(\rho_t\) and multiplied, a sample whose probability had been wrongly pushed up would contribute nothing, and the mistake would go uncorrected.

Be clear about what this is not. It is an incentive structure, not a constraint. Nothing in \(L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}\) makes it impossible to leave the window: a large learning rate, or too many passes over one batch, can carry \(\theta\) far past \(1 \pm \epsilon\), because a sample's gradient vanishes only once you are already outside. That is why you still monitor the KL.

3.4.5 The full loss, and one iteration of code

Because the clip keeps updates near \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\), PPO can safely reuse a batch: collect a rollout of \(N\) steps from several parallel environments, then run several epochs — complete passes over the stored batch — of minibatch gradient descent before collecting fresh data. That reuse is where most of PPO's sample efficiency comes from. The policy term is not the whole loss, though:

\[ L^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta,\phi) \;=\; -\,L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) \;+\; c_v\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\big(V_\phi(s_t) - \hat{G}_t\big)^2\right] \;-\; c_e\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\mathcal{H}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\big)\right] \]
In plain words
This is what the optimizer minimizes, so signs flip relative to the objective. \(\theta\) are the policy's parameters, \(\phi\) the critic's. The first term is minus the clipped surrogate: minimizing it maximizes \(L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}\). The second is the value loss — squared error between the critic's prediction \(V_\phi(s_t)\) and the observed return target \(\hat{G}_t\) — which trains the critic the advantage estimator needs. The third is the entropy bonus: \(\mathcal{H}\) is the entropy of the action distribution, high when the policy is spread out, near zero when nearly deterministic. It enters with a minus sign, so minimizing the loss increases entropy, paying the policy to stay random. \(c_v\) and \(c_e\) are scalar weights, commonly 0.5 and 0.01.

Where does \(\hat{G}_t\) come from? Not from a second pass over the episode. Once Section 3.5 hands you \(\hat{A}_t\), the target is just \(\hat{G}_t = \hat{A}_t + V(s_t)\) — the critic's own estimate, corrected by the advantage you measured. That identity is what makes the critic loss and the advantage estimator one machine instead of two, and it is what fills the returns field below (and rollout_buffer.returns in Stable-Baselines3).

# One PPO iteration: collect once, then reuse the batch several times.
batch = collect_rollout(pi_old, n_envs=8, n_steps=2048)   # 16384 steps total
adv   = gae(batch.rewards, batch.values, gamma, lam)      # Section 3.5
batch.returns = adv + batch.values                        # critic targets: G = A + V
batch.adv     = (adv - adv.mean()) / (adv.std() + 1e-8)   # per-batch normalization
for epoch in range(4):                                    # 4 passes over the SAME data
    for mb in minibatches(batch, size=512):
        # ratio in log-space; mb.logp_old was stored during collection,
        # so no frozen copy of the old network is needed here
        rho   = exp(logp(pi, mb.a) - mb.logp_old)
        # min() of unclipped and clipped: take the pessimistic branch
        l_pi  = -min(rho * mb.adv,
                     clip(rho, 1-eps, 1+eps) * mb.adv).mean()
        l_v   = (v(mb.s) - mb.returns).pow(2).mean()      # critic regression
        loss  = l_pi + 0.5 * l_v - 0.01 * entropy(pi, mb.s)
        loss.backward(); opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()
pi_old = pi                                               # snapshot for the next iteration

PPO became the field's default for an unglamorous reason: it is a first-order method, roughly a dozen lines on top of an actor-critic, robust across a wide range of hyperparameters, and it scales by adding parallel environments. It gives up TRPO's guarantees and keeps most of its results.

But notice what it needs. Every line above depends on an exact, differentiable \(\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\) for each sampled action. Chapter 7 shows that flow-matching VLAs cannot provide one, and Chapter 9 works through the consequences side by side with everything here. That single missing quantity is why the LeHome solution left PPO behind entirely.

Words you will hear
Clip fraction — the fraction of samples in a minibatch where \(\rho_t\) fell outside \([1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]\). Approximate KL (approx_kl) — a cheap sample estimate of the KL between old and new policy, used for monitoring and early stopping. Explained variance — \(1 - \mathrm{Var}(\hat{G}-V)/\mathrm{Var}(\hat{G})\); near 1 means a useful critic, near or below 0 a useless one. Adaptive KL penalty — the other PPO variant from the same paper: subtract \(\beta_{KL} D_{KL}\) from the objective you maximize, equivalently add \(+\beta_{KL} D_{KL}\) to the loss you minimize, with \(\beta_{KL}\) retuned between iterations to hold the measured KL near a target. The subscript keeps it apart from the AWR temperature \(\beta\) later in the book. Rarely used today. Epoch / minibatch — one full pass over the stored rollout, and one slice of it.

3.5 Advantage estimation: from Monte Carlo to TD to GAE

The clipped objective consumes \(\hat{A}_t\) and does nothing to check it. Whatever quality that estimate has, the learning inherits. So where does it come from? Two pure answers exist, and they fail in opposite directions.

3.5.1 Monte Carlo: unbiased and nearly unusable

The direct approach: run the episode to the end, add up the discounted rewards you actually received, subtract the baseline. That is \(\hat{A}_t = G_t - V(s_t)\) with \(G_t = \sum_k \gamma^k r_{t+k}\). Nothing is approximated — \(G_t\) is what happened. The estimate is unbiased.

Here is the concrete failure. On your rover, consider the first action of an episode, a small steering command. Two episodes start from nearly the same state and take nearly the same first action. In one, the randomized obstacle layout leaves a clear lane and the rover reaches the goal for \(G_0 = +180\). In the other an obstacle sits square in the path, the rover detours, runs out of time, and \(G_0 = -20\). The same action is scored at \(+180\) in one sample and \(-20\) in the other, and the difference has nothing to do with the action. \(G_t\) sums every random event between \(t\) and the end of the episode and charges all of it to \(a_t\). The variance is enormous and grows with episode length.

3.5.2 TD: low variance and wrong

The opposite approach: do not wait. Take one real reward, then ask the critic what everything after is worth. That gives the TD residual, the one-step temporal-difference error:

\[ \delta_t \;=\; r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t). \]

In words: the immediate reward, plus the discounted value the critic assigns to where you landed, minus the value it assigned to where you started. If the transition went better than the critic expected, \(\delta_t\) is positive. Only one step of real randomness enters, so variance is small and does not grow with the episode.

Here is its concrete failure. Early in training your critic is a barely-trained network outputting numbers near zero everywhere. Your rover takes the action that turns it toward the goal, moving from a state genuinely worth \(0.9\) to one genuinely worth \(0.95\). The reward on this step is \(0\), because reward is sparse. The critic reports \(V(s_t) = 0.02\) and \(V(s_{t+1}) = 0.01\). Then \(\delta_t = 0 + 0.99 \times 0.01 - 0.02 = -0.01\): slightly negative. The estimator says a good action was mildly bad. That is not noise you can average away — run it a thousand times and the sign stays wrong, because the error lives in \(V\), not in the sampling. That is bias.

A software analogy: MC times a request end-to-end with a stopwatch; TD reads a cost estimate off a cached model of how long each function takes. The stopwatch is honest, but every measurement is polluted by whatever else the machine was doing. The cost model is stable and, if stale, gives the same wrong number every time. Where it breaks down: a profiler's cost model is calibrated independently of the request you are timing, while the critic is trained on the very returns you are estimating, so its errors and your data's errors are correlated.

3.5.3 The dial between them

Between the extremes sit \(k\)-step estimators: sum \(k\) real rewards, then bootstrap with \(\gamma^k V(s_{t+k})\). Larger \(k\) means more real signal and more variance; smaller \(k\) means more reliance on the critic and more bias. Picking a single \(k\) is a brittle decision. Generalized Advantage Estimation (Schulman et al., 2016) sidesteps it by blending all values of \(k\) at once with exponentially decaying weights:

\[ \hat{A}_t^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} \;=\; \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l}, \qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t). \]
In plain words
To score the action at time \(t\), walk forward through the rest of the episode collecting TD residuals: \(\delta_t\) from this step, \(\delta_{t+1}\) from the next, and so on, where each \(\delta\) is the reward \(r\) plus the discounted next value minus the current one. \(l\) counts how many steps ahead you have walked. Each residual is multiplied by \((\gamma\lambda)^l\), a number below 1 raised to the \(l\)-th power, so residuals further ahead count exponentially less. \(\gamma\) is the discount from the problem definition; \(\lambda\), the GAE parameter, lies in \([0,1]\) and sets how fast that decay happens. At \(\lambda = 0\) only the first term survives: pure TD. At \(\lambda = 1\) nothing decays beyond \(\gamma\) and the sum telescopes into \(G_t - V(s_t)\): pure Monte Carlo. Everything between is a blend.

Figure 3.2 draws \(\lambda\) as the dial it is. In practice \(\lambda \approx 0.95\) sits close to the MC end. Read it as: trust the real rewards for roughly the next twenty steps, then hand off to the critic. Note also that the implementation is one backward pass, not an infinite sum — accumulate \(\hat{A}_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda \hat{A}_{t+1}\) from the end of the rollout to the start, which costs essentially nothing.

3.5.4 \(\gamma\) and \(\lambda\) are not the same knob

They look alike in the formula, they multiply together, and both are slightly below 1. Everything else about them differs, and conflating them is a common confusion.

\(\gamma\) belongs to the problem definition. It is part of the return \(G_t\) you decided to maximize back in Chapter 1, and it says how much the agent should care about a reward twenty seconds from now relative to one right now. Change \(\gamma\) and you change what the optimal policy is: a \(\gamma = 0.9\) rover is a more impatient animal than a \(\gamma = 0.999\) rover, and the two behave differently at convergence. Tune \(\gamma\) to the task's time horizon.

\(\lambda\) belongs to the estimator only and appears nowhere in the objective. It trades bias against variance in the numbers you feed the gradient, and with a perfect critic every \(\lambda\) estimates the same quantity with different noise; the optimal policy is unchanged. Tune \(\lambda\) to how much you trust your critic — young critic or sparse reward, push it up; well-fit critic and long noisy episodes, pull it down.

3.5.5 Worked example: three estimators, one three-step episode

Take a deliberately tiny episode so every number is checkable by hand. Three steps; \(\gamma = 0.9\); rewards \(r_0 = 0\), \(r_1 = 0\), \(r_2 = 1\) — success only at the end, the sparse-reward situation you face on a robot. The critic, mid-training and mediocre, reports \(V(s_0) = 0.5\), \(V(s_1) = 0.6\), \(V(s_2) = 0.7\), and \(V(s_3) = 0\) because \(s_3\) is terminal.

First the three TD residuals, \(\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)\), every multiplication shown:

\[ \delta_0 = 0 + 0.9 \times 0.6 - 0.5 = 0 + 0.54 - 0.5 = 0.04 \]

\[ \delta_1 = 0 + 0.9 \times 0.7 - 0.6 = 0 + 0.63 - 0.6 = 0.03 \]

\[ \delta_2 = 1 + 0.9 \times 0 - 0.7 = 1 + 0 - 0.7 = 0.30 \]

With \(\lambda = 0\) the sum keeps only its first term, so the advantages are the residuals themselves: \(\hat{A}_0 = 0.04\), \(\hat{A}_1 = 0.03\), \(\hat{A}_2 = 0.30\). Read what that says. The success at \(t=2\) gives a healthy signal for the last action, but the first action gets \(0.04\) — almost nothing. The estimator refuses to look past one step; it credits the first action only for the critic's opinion that \(s_1\) beats \(s_0\), and that opinion is worth \(0.04\).

With \(\lambda = 0.95\) the decay factor is \(\gamma\lambda = 0.9 \times 0.95 = 0.855\), and we accumulate backward from the end:

A_2 = d_2                        = 0.30
A_1 = d_1 + 0.855 * A_2
    = 0.03 + 0.855 * 0.30
    = 0.03 + 0.2565             = 0.2865   ~  0.287
A_0 = d_0 + 0.855 * A_1
    = 0.04 + 0.855 * 0.2865
    = 0.04 + 0.24496            = 0.28496  ~  0.285

Read this one too. The terminal success now reaches the first action almost undamped: \(0.285\) versus \(0.04\), same action, same data, same critic. That is a factor of just over seven in the learning signal — \(0.285 / 0.04 = 7.125\) — and the only thing that changed was one number in the config file.

With \(\lambda = 1\) the decay factor is \(\gamma \lambda = 0.9 \times 1 = 0.9\), giving \(\hat{A}_1 = 0.03 + 0.9 \times 0.30 = 0.03 + 0.27 = 0.30\) and \(\hat{A}_0 = 0.04 + 0.9 \times 0.30 = 0.04 + 0.27 = 0.31\). Check that against the Monte Carlo definition: \(G_0 = 0 + 0.9 \times 0 + 0.9^2 \times 1 = 0.81\), so \(\hat{A}_0 = G_0 - V(s_0) = 0.81 - 0.5 = 0.31\). The two routes agree — the telescoping property in action.

So \(\lambda = 0.95\) recovers \(0.285\) of the \(0.31\) full Monte Carlo gives, while still damping the far tail. On a three-step episode that damping buys little; on a 500-step episode it is the difference between a usable signal and noise. Figure 3.3 puts all three side by side.

3.6 PPO in practice on the rover

Everything so far has been mechanism. This section is what you actually do with it on rl-rover: a starting configuration, and the four plots that tell you whether the run is healthy.

3.6.1 A configuration to start from

On a task the size of rl-rover, a sane configuration is 8 parallel environments, 2048 steps each per rollout (a 16,384-step batch), 4 epochs of minibatch updates, \(\gamma = 0.99\), \(\lambda = 0.95\), \(\epsilon = 0.2\), and a budget of roughly 5M environment steps. Those are numbers to start from, not numbers to tune first. Two of them repay a second look. The 4 epochs are the reuse factor of Section 3.4: raise it and you extract more from each batch, but you also travel further from \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\) within one iteration. The \(\lambda = 0.95\) is the dial of Section 3.5, sitting near the Monte Carlo end because your critic starts out untrained.

3.6.2 The four curves

Four TensorBoard curves tell you nearly everything. Read them as a set; their failure modes are joint.

Episode reward (ep_rew_mean) is the ground truth. Everything else exists to explain this curve. If it is going up, resist the urge to fix things.

Approximate KL and clip fraction tell you how far updates actually move the policy. Healthy is a KL in the \(0.01\)–\(0.03\) band with clip fraction around \(0.1\)–\(0.3\). Clip fraction near zero means the clip never binds: either the learning rate is too timid or learning has converged, and the reward curve tells you which. KL spiking with clip fraction above \(0.4\) is the danger sign of Section 3.1. Lower the learning rate, or cut the epochs per batch.

Value loss should fall early, then track changes in reward rather than sitting flat. If it stays large, check explained variance: a critic near zero there contributes bias and nothing else.

Entropy should decay slowly and smoothly. A cliff-drop in the first 10% of training means the policy went deterministic before it found anything worth being confident about; raise the entropy coefficient. Entropy that climbs steadily usually means a broken advantage signal.

On your rig
In Stable-Baselines3 these knobs are n_envs, n_steps, n_epochs, clip_range, gae_lambda, ent_coef; the curves are approx_kl, clip_fraction, entropy_loss, value_loss, explained_variance. Re-run rl-rover and watch clip_fraction during the reward ramp — it rises exactly when the policy changes fastest, the most direct view of this chapter you will get. If a sim-to-real transfer got worse after a "better" run, check whether entropy collapsed early; overconfident policies randomize poorly and transfer poorly. Your hil-serl SO-101 setup uses an off-policy learner, so there is no clip_fraction there, but the critic diagnostics carry over.
Pitfalls
Two silent killers. (1) Skipping per-batch advantage normalization: raw \(\hat{A}\) magnitudes drift over training, so the effective learning rate drifts with them. Normalize advantages to zero mean and unit variance per batch — but never normalize rewards mid-run with running statistics you later change, or the critic's targets shift underneath it. (2) Reward scaling: multiplying rewards by 100 multiplies the value loss by 10,000 and its gradients by 100. If the value loss dwarfs the policy loss, the shared trunk trains the critic and ignores the actor; rescale the rewards or lower the value coefficient \(c_v\).
In the LeHome solution
PPO never appears. A flow-matching VLA has no tractable \(\log \pi_\theta(a \mid o)\), so the ratio \(\rho_t\) simply cannot be computed (Chapter 7; Chapter 9 makes the comparison explicit). But Section 3.5 survives completely intact. Larchenko's advantage pipeline uses TD residuals, Monte Carlo returns, and GAE computed over checkpoint rewards, with the learned success-probability head \(\hat{p}_{succ}\) standing in for \(V\). Because that value estimate is imperfect, the full subtraction is replaced by a damped baseline \(\alpha V\), where the damping coefficient \(\alpha\) sits below 1 and deliberately subtracts less than the whole value estimate (Chapter 11). The estimator outlived the algorithm that made it famous (Larchenko, 2026).
