Chapter 9 — PPO versus VLA-RL: the two worlds side by side

9.1 Why this chapter exists

You have trained PPO and it worked. You wrote a Gymnasium environment for a differential-drive rover, let Stable-Baselines3 run for a few million steps, watched the return curve climb, added domain randomization, and transferred the policy to a 3D-printed robot. That means you know something people usually only read about: what it feels like when an on-policy algorithm is learning, when it is stuck, and when it has quietly diverged.

Then you open a modern manipulation paper — \(\pi_{0.5}\) (Physical Intelligence, 2025a), \(\pi^*_{0.6}\) and RECAP, its recipe for reinforcement learning from experience and corrections (Physical Intelligence, 2025b), the LeHome solution (Larchenko, 2026) — and almost nothing you recognize is there. No clipped surrogate. No importance ratio. No entropy bonus. No rollout buffer flushed after four epochs. Instead: a flow-matching action expert, a replay pool — a persistent store of past episodes that the trainer draws from again and again — with a decay factor on the old ones, a success head that doubles as a critic, an advantage token, a bandit tuning guidance at inference. It is easy to conclude you have been learning the wrong thing.

You have not. But the two worlds really are different, and the difference is not cosmetic — it is forced by the policy class and by the cost of a sample. This chapter puts them side by side, arrow for arrow and symbol for symbol, so that when you read a paper you know which world you are standing in, what the authors could not have done, and why they did what they did instead.

Words you will hear

On-policy — the update uses only data generated by the policy being updated, right now. Off-policy — it uses data from some other policy: an old checkpoint, a human, a script. Staleness — how many checkpoints old a sample is; stale means more off-policy. KL divergence — a single number measuring how far one probability distribution sits from another; a trust region is a cap on it. GRPO — group-relative policy optimization: a policy-gradient method that drops the critic and scores each rollout against the average return of a group of sibling rollouts from the same start. Classifier-free guidance — generating twice, once with the conditioning and once without, then extrapolating away from the unconditioned result to sharpen the sample toward the condition.

9.2 The two loops, drawn

Start with the shapes of the two training loops, because almost every other difference follows from them. Figure 9.1 draws both. Take them one arrow at a time.

9.2.1 Walking the PPO loop

Arrow one: you run the current policy \(\pi_\theta\) for a fixed number of steps and store the transitions, which belong to \(\pi_\theta\) and to nothing else. Arrow two: you sweep backwards through the batch computing TD residuals \(\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)\) and folding them into \(\hat{A}_t\) with the GAE parameter \(\lambda\). Arrow three: several gradient epochs over that same batch, during which the policy drifts away from the one that collected the data — exactly what the clip parameter \(\epsilon\) exists to bound. Arrow four: you drop the batch on the floor, because after a few epochs \(\rho_t(\theta)\) is no longer a small correction, and the surrogate objective — the function you actually differentiate, standing in for the expected return, which samples alone give you no usable gradient of — has stopped being a faithful proxy.

The critical property is that this loop is a barrier: collection cannot overlap the update, because the update changes the policy that collection needs. In your rover project that was invisible, because a simulator step cost microseconds and the loop spun thousands of times per minute.

9.2.2 Walking the VLA-RL loop

Now the right panel. Arrow one: several rollout workers run some checkpoint — not necessarily the newest — and append finished episodes to a store on disk. Arrow two: human demonstrations, replayed episodes and DAgger corrections — dataset aggregation, meaning you run the current policy, a human corrects it while it runs, and the corrected frames join the pool (Chapter 13) — flow into the same store from a different pipeline. Arrow three: before each training iteration, a job walks the whole pool and computes an advantage for every frame from the policy's own value-like head. Arrow four: the trainer samples with probabilities that depend on those advantages and takes ordinary supervised steps. Arrow five, dashed: a new checkpoint is published, and workers pick it up whenever they finish an episode.

Nobody waits for anybody. In LeHome an Isaac Sim episode takes about 30 seconds, while the trainer publishes a checkpoint roughly every 40 minutes on a single H200 (Larchenko, 2026). Locked together, those two rates would idle the GPU for most of the run. PPO's loop is a pipeline in which each stage invalidates the previous stage's output; this one is a producer/consumer system around a durable dataset. Once you have that store, every other design decision changes.

9.3 Component by component

Now the machinery, piece by piece. For each: what PPO does, what VLA-RL does, and — the part that matters — why the change was forced rather than chosen.

9.3.1 (a) The policy class

PPO's policy is a small network emitting a mean and a log standard deviation; the action is one draw from that Gaussian at one timestep. It is cheap, it has a closed-form density, and it is unimodal. VLA-RL's policy is a vision-language backbone plus a flow-matching action expert that generates a whole chunk \(a_{t:t+H}\) — in LeHome, 30 timesteps of 12 joint deltas at once — by integrating a learned velocity field \(v_\theta(a^u, u, o)\) from noise over flow time \(u \in [0,1]\) in \(K\) steps.

Why forced: Chapter 5 showed that a single Gaussian cannot represent "go left around the obstacle or right around it, but never straight through," and manipulation is full of such choices. But the machinery of Chapters 6 and 7 buys that expressiveness by giving up the one thing PPO needs most.

9.3.2 (b) What the update actually consumes

This is the hinge of the whole chapter, so slow down here. PPO's objective is

\[ L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\Big[\min\big(\rho_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t,\; \operatorname{clip}\big(\rho_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\big)\,\hat{A}_t\big)\Big], \qquad \rho_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)}. \]
In plain words

The left side, \(L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)\), is the clipped surrogate objective: the score we push upward by gradient ascent. \(\mathbb{E}_t\) averages over every timestep in the batch. \(\rho_t(\theta)\) is how much more likely the policy we are building now is to have taken the action actually taken than the policy that collected it: 1.0 unchanged, 1.4 forty percent more likely. \(\hat{A}_t\) is how much better than average that action turned out, so the product says "make good actions more likely, in proportion to how good they were." \(\operatorname{clip}\) flattens the ratio outside \(1 \pm \epsilon\) (typically \(\epsilon = 0.2\), SB3's default), and \(\min\) takes the smaller product, so once the policy has moved far enough in the direction this sample asked for — a good action already more than \(1+\epsilon\) times as likely, a bad one already below \(1-\epsilon\) — the sample stops pulling. The reverse case is deliberately not capped: if the ratio runs the wrong way, the sample keeps pulling at full strength.

Every quantity there is one line of code for a Gaussian. So the obvious thing to try is to port it: keep the loop, swap the Gaussian head for the flow-matching expert, compute \(\rho_t\), go. Here is exactly why that fails.

Start with what a flow policy is. It is a sampler, not a formula. You hand it an observation, it draws noise \(z\), integrates the velocity field for \(K\) steps, and gives back a chunk. What you cannot do is hand it a chunk and ask "how probable was that?" — the number \(\pi_\theta(a \mid o)\) is never computed anywhere in that process. The distinction is familiar from your own code: a torch distribution object gives you both sample() and log_prob(), and PPO leans on the second every epoch. A flow policy ships only the first, and the missing one is not a gap in the library — for this class of model it is a hard integral.

The integral can be written down, and Chapter 7 §7.6 prices it in full; Figure 7.3 is the anatomy. The short version: recovering \(\log \pi_\theta(a \mid o)\) from a flow means integrating the divergence of the velocity field along the generation path, which costs about 3,600 backward passes through the action expert per log-probability — for one sample, at one gradient step, against one forward pass for the supervised alternative. The cheap approximation makes it worse rather than better: a stochastic trace estimate leaves noise in log space that the ratio exponentiates, and a wobble of \(\pm 0.7\) moves \(\rho_t\) between 0.5 and 2.0 while PPO's whole clip window at \(\epsilon = 0.2\) is 0.8 to 1.2. The noise would not perturb the objective; it would choose it.

What matters for this comparison is the shape of the requirement, not the size of the bill. PPO's update consumes a density: it must evaluate how probable a specific already-chosen action was, under two different parameter settings, differentiably. That is a strong demand, and a Gaussian meets it only because its density happens to have a closed form. Weighted imitation consumes something far weaker — a recorded action, a loss that can reproduce it, and one scalar per sample. Every architectural difference in the rest of this section follows from that gap. A policy class chosen for the quality of its samples cannot also be asked for its densities, and once you accept that, you stop trying to compute a ratio and start asking which data to imitate and how loudly.

Now suppose the exact log-density were free. A second barrier remains, and it is not about compute. Half of any policy gradient is push-away: when \(\hat{A}_t\) is negative, the update lowers the probability of the action just produced. For a Gaussian that is harmless — the mean slides, the distribution stays a distribution. For a flow policy, the chunks that make sense occupy a thin, curved manifold inside those 360 dimensions: joint-delta sequences that are smooth, reachable and physically coherent. Most directions that lower one chunk's probability point off that manifold, so the update produces not a different sensible motion but an incoherent one, and the damage shows up in everything the network generates afterwards. Hence (Larchenko, 2026) gives two reasons, not one: log-prob policy gradients do not transfer cleanly, and push-away updates shove predictions off the narrow action manifold. Weighted imitation never takes such a step, because every gradient it takes says "reproduce this recorded chunk."

So the ratio is off the table, and with it the clipped objective, the log-prob KL penalty, the entropy bonus, and GRPO-style objectives too (group-relative policy optimization scores a rollout against a group of sibling rollouts instead of a critic, but it still needs \(\log \pi_\theta\)). What is left is something the flow policy is extremely good at: being told what to imitate. The Chapter 4 objective — advantage-weighted regression, or AWR — is

\[ \max_\theta \; \sum_i w_i \, \log \pi_\theta(a_i \mid o_i), \qquad w_i = \exp\!\big(\hat{A}_i / \beta\big). \]
In plain words

Sum over samples \(i\) drawn from whatever data you have. \(\log \pi_\theta(a_i \mid o_i)\) is the ordinary behavior-cloning term: "make the action \(a_i\) that was taken in observation \(o_i\) more likely." \(w_i\) is a non-negative number scaling how hard you pull on that sample. \(\hat{A}_i\) is the same advantage estimate as before; \(\beta\) is a temperature, small \(\beta\) sharpening the exponential so only the best samples matter, large \(\beta\) flattening it toward plain cloning. Notice what is gone: no old policy, no ratio, no second network to evaluate. Notice also what is still sitting there: \(\log \pi_\theta\), the density under the network we are training — the very thing just declared unaffordable. Resolving that is the next four paragraphs.

So look hard at what this objective asks for. It must make the recorded chunk \(a_i\) more likely under the current network, and pull harder when \(w_i\) is large. Read literally, that means computing \(\log \pi_\theta(a_i \mid o_i)\) — the quantity we just spent four paragraphs proving unaffordable. It looks like we have walked in a circle.

We have not, and the reason is the load-bearing step of this whole family of methods. Gradient descent never uses the value of a loss term; it uses the gradient. The optimizer does not care what \(\log \pi_\theta(a_i \mid o_i)\) equals. It needs only a direction in weight space that raises the likelihood of \(a_i\) given \(o_i\), scaled by \(w_i\). A ratio is a different animal: \(\rho_t\) is a number that gets divided, clipped and compared against \(1 \pm \epsilon\), none of which is possible without the value. Ratios need values; likelihood terms need only directions. That asymmetry is why one of these objectives survives the move to flow matching and the other does not.

And a direction that raises the likelihood of \(a_i\) is exactly what flow matching hands you. Its loss asks the network, at every point along the path from noise to action, to predict the velocity that carries noise toward this chunk given this observation, so descending it makes the network more likely to generate \(a_i\) from \(o_i\). So we substitute: wherever AWR calls for a step that raises \(\log \pi_\theta(a_i \mid o_i)\) in proportion to \(w_i\), take a step that lowers the flow-matching loss on \((o_i, a_i)\) with that same \(w_i\).

Two consequences. First, the sign flips: AWR maximizes a log-likelihood, flow matching minimizes a squared error, and raising one while lowering the other moves the weights the same way — so \(\max\) becomes \(\min\), and \(w_i\) multiplies a squared error instead of a log-probability. Second, be honest about the claim. The flow-matching loss is not equal to \(-\log \pi_\theta(a_i \mid o_i)\), and nothing above proves it is; it is a surrogate, a different function whose gradient points the same way. The equality is never needed, because the number was never needed — only the direction. Here is that loss, from Chapter 7:

\[ \min_\theta \; L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{i,\,u,\,z}\Big[\, w_i \,\big\| v_\theta\big(a_i^u,\, u,\, o_i,\, c_i\big) - (a_i - z) \big\|^2 \Big], \qquad a_i^u = (1-u)\,z + u\,a_i . \]
In plain words

Pick a sample \(i\) from the pool. Draw a flow time \(u\) uniformly in \([0,1]\) and a noise chunk \(z\) from a standard Gaussian. \(a_i\) is the action chunk that was executed; \(a_i^u\) is the straight-line blend between pure noise and that chunk, a fraction \(u\) along. \(v_\theta\) is the network's guess at the direction that moves from the blend toward the real chunk, given the observation \(o_i\) and the conditioning \(c_i\) — typically an advantage token, an input that tells the model during training how well this chunk turned out, so that at inference you can ask it for a good one (Chapter 10). The target \(a_i - z\) is the true direction of that line, the loss is the squared error between guess and target averaged over samples, flow times and noise draws, and the AWR weight \(w_i\) scales each sample's contribution. This is supervised learning; the only trace of RL is which samples get drawn, how heavily each counts, and what \(c_i\) says about them.

Figure 9.2 lays the two ingredient lists side by side. The crossed-out box on the right is the whole content of this section.

9.3.3 (c) Where the data comes from

PPO consumes fresh on-policy data and nothing else; a batch from three updates ago is not suboptimal, it is invalid input to the surrogate. VLA-RL consumes a mixed pool: rollouts from dozens of past checkpoints, teleoperation, DAgger corrections, curriculum-selected episodes, replays from saved physics snapshots. Why forced: when an episode costs 30 seconds of simulator time or two minutes of a human's attention, discarding it after one update is unaffordable. The pool is not a compromise; it becomes the primary object you engineer (Chapter 14).

9.3.4 (d) Staying close to something

Both worlds know an unconstrained update destroys a policy, but they constrain different things. PPO keeps the policy near its own previous version, using the clip on \(\rho_t\) as a cheap stand-in for a KL trust region — a cap on how far one update may move the policy, measured as a KL divergence in probability space (Schulman et al., 2015; Schulman et al., 2017). Weighted BC keeps it near the data. Every gradient it takes has the form "reproduce this recorded chunk, harder or softer," so all such an update can do is redistribute probability mass among the actions the policy already produces with non-negligible probability; it never pushes mass toward a region the policy currently treats as essentially impossible. The temperature \(\beta\) is the leash length, and in the derivation it comes from a KL constraint to the behavior policy \(\pi_\beta\) (Peng et al., 2019; Nair et al., 2020).

An analogy, and its limit. PPO's trust region is a leash tied to where you stood a moment ago: any direction, just not far. Weighted BC's constraint is a fence around the ground the policy can already reach: anywhere inside, instantly, but nothing outside. There is real room inside that fence: a flow policy interpolates, which is exactly why sampling temperature, guidance and best-of-\(N\) change anything at all. Limited exploration here means narrow, not zero. Where the analogy breaks: the fence moves. Your own rollouts keep entering the pool, so the enclosed ground creeps outward into whatever the policy has recently managed to do — which is why these methods drift rather than jump, and why LeHome needed deliberate checkpoint rollbacks to escape a local optimum the fence had grown around.

9.3.5 (e) The value function

PPO trains a separate critic \(V_\phi(s)\), usually a twin of the policy trunk, by squared-error regression to returns; \(\phi\) is a second set of weights, updated alongside \(\theta\) but never shared with it. VLA-RL cannot afford a second three-billion-parameter model and does not need one: Chapter 8 showed that a linear head on the policy's own query token predicts \(\hat{p}_{succ}\), the probability that the episode ends in success.

A value then follows by subtraction, \(V(s_t) = \hat{p}_{succ} - R_{acc}\), and that identity deserves a moment because it is not obvious. Under LeHome's scoring, once the withdrawal rule for failed episodes is applied, an episode's total reward is effectively binary, so \(\hat{p}_{succ}\) — the head's probability that this episode ends in success — is its expected total reward. \(R_{acc}\) is the part of that total already banked at the checkpoints passed so far, and a value is by definition the reward still ahead. Expected total minus already collected leaves what remains: that is the whole derivation, and Chapter 11 uses the damped version of it. Why forced: cost — with a bonus, since a head sharing the backbone sees exactly the representation the policy acts on, so critic and actor cannot disagree about what they are looking at.

9.3.6 (f) The advantage estimate

PPO computes \(\hat{A}_t\) with GAE inside the current batch, using \(\gamma\) and \(\lambda\), normalized per minibatch. VLA-RL computes GAE too, with identical mechanics — the single largest piece of your PPO knowledge that survives intact — but four things around it change. First, it runs offline, over the entire pool, before each training iteration, instead of inline over one fresh batch. Second, the baseline comes from the success head rather than a critic, and it is multiplied by a damping coefficient \(\alpha\) that shrinks it toward zero, because a baseline that is itself noisy adds variance instead of removing it. Third, a completion head supplies potential-based shaping: extra per-step reward built from a function of the state, in a form that provably cannot change which policy is optimal. Fourth, as an episode ages its per-frame value predictions — made by a checkpoint since replaced — become less trustworthy, so the estimate fades toward a GRPO-style group anchor, which compares whole episodes against the average outcome of a group rather than trusting any per-frame number. Why forced: staleness, in a form PPO never has to think about. Chapter 11 is the full treatment.

9.3.7 (g) Exploration

PPO explores by sampling from the Gaussian and by an entropy bonus that pays the policy to keep its standard deviation up. In VLA-RL there is no entropy term to add, and as 9.3(d) said, the update can only shift mass among behaviors the policy already produces. Exploration therefore moves to the data pipeline — DAgger corrections, hard-state mining (deliberately collecting more episodes from the states where the policy currently fails), replays with heavier augmentation — and to inference: noise temperature on the initial \(z\), the classifier-free guidance scale \(g\) (generate once with the conditioning and once without, then extrapolate away from the unconditioned direction by a factor \(g\), which sharpens the sample toward the condition), and best-of-\(N\) scored by a learned head (Chapter 12). Why forced: the same missing density. The cost is real: this family explores weakly, and every serious write-up says so.

9.3.8 (h) Synchronization

PPO is a barrier; VLA-RL is a set of processes sharing a filesystem. In LeHome, trainer, rollout workers and DAgger station communicate only through a Hugging Face Hub repository, with no synchronization primitives at all. Why forced: heterogeneous hardware and timescales. Adding a worker machine should not require touching the trainer, and here it does not.

9.3.9 (i) Where the improvement actually comes from

This is the deepest difference and the easiest to miss. PPO improves the policy by ascending a gradient; the mechanism is optimization. Weighted BC and conditioning improve it by changing which data the supervised loss sees and then querying the resulting model well; the mechanism is re-weighting, re-labeling and clever sampling. Hence the knobs in these papers: per-source shares, decay factors, sampling priorities, curricula, guidance, best-of-\(N\), execution length — data and inference knobs, not optimizer knobs.

A second analogy, and its limit. PPO is like tuning a live service by shipping a change, measuring it against fresh traffic, and discarding the measurement the moment you ship again. VLA-RL is like curating a training corpus in version control: keep everything, label each example with how well it went, oversample the good ones, let old entries decay, retrain. Where it breaks: a corpus is static, but this one is written by the model's own past checkpoints, so a bug you introduce today can poison the pool for weeks.

9.3.10 A worked example: one transition, two updates

Take a single scalar action — say a gripper aperture delta. The pre-update Gaussian policy has mean \(\mu_{old} = 0.10\) and standard deviation \(\sigma = 0.20\). The action taken was \(a_t = 0.34\), and GAE says \(\hat{A}_t = +1.2\). One gradient step later the mean has moved to \(\mu_{new} = 0.16\), \(\sigma\) unchanged. Here is the PPO arithmetic, every line:

z_old = (0.34 - 0.10)/0.20 = 1.20        # standardized action, old policy
log pi_old = -0.5*(1.20^2) - ln(0.20) - 0.5*ln(2*pi)
           = -0.720 + 1.6094 - 0.9189 = -0.0295

z_new = (0.34 - 0.16)/0.20 = 0.90        # standardized action, new policy
log pi_new = -0.5*(0.90^2) - ln(0.20) - 0.5*ln(2*pi)
           = -0.405 + 1.6094 - 0.9189 = +0.2855

rho  = exp(0.2855 - (-0.0295)) = exp(0.3150) = 1.3703
clip(rho, 0.8, 1.2) = 1.2000             # eps = 0.2
unclipped term = 1.3703 * 1.2 = 1.6443
clipped   term = 1.2000 * 1.2 = 1.4400
objective = min(1.6443, 1.4400) = 1.4400 -> the CLIPPED branch wins
                                          -> gradient from this sample is zero

The mechanism is visible: the policy has already moved more than \(1+\epsilon\) in the direction this sample was asking for, so PPO switches the sample off. (Had \(\hat{A}_t\) been negative with the same ratio, \(\min\) would have picked the unclipped branch and the sample would still be pulling.) Every number above needed \(\log \pi\).

Now the other world, after one preliminary. The equation above puts \(w_i\) on the loss; the code below puts it in the sampler. Those are the same thing in expectation — drawing a sample twice as often and multiplying its loss by two produce the same average gradient — and the sampler version is strictly cheaper on a robot pool, because a frame that is never drawn is never decoded, never loaded, and never pushed through a three-billion-parameter backbone. So the arithmetic below computes weights and then reads them as sampling probabilities.

Take a pool of four frames with advantages \(+1.2, +0.3, -0.4, -1.5\) and \(\beta = 0.5\), and sample proportionally to \(w_i = \exp(\hat{A}_i/\beta)\):

w1 = exp(+1.2/0.5) = exp(+2.4) = 11.0232
w2 = exp(+0.3/0.5) = exp(+0.6) =  1.8221
w3 = exp(-0.4/0.5) = exp(-0.8) =  0.4493
w4 = exp(-1.5/0.5) = exp(-3.0) =  0.0498
sum                            = 13.3444

p1 = 11.0232/13.3444 = 0.8261     p2 = 1.8221/13.3444 = 0.1365
p3 =  0.4493/13.3444 = 0.0337     p4 = 0.0498/13.3444 = 0.0037

Frame 1 is drawn 83 percent of the time; the badly-failing frame 4 once in 270 draws. LeHome does not tune \(\beta\) at all — it fixes \(\beta = 1\) and clips \(\hat{A}_i\) into \([-2, 2]\) before exponentiating, so the heaviest frame can be at most \(e^{4} \approx 55\) times the lightest, which is what stops one lucky episode from owning every batch.

Say frame 1 is drawn. It is the same transition as the PPO example above: the same gripper-aperture action \(a_i = 0.34\) in the same observation \(o_i\) — camera images and joint states, which the network consumes but which never show up in the arithmetic — which is why its advantage is again \(+1.2\). Real chunks are 360 numbers wide; a single scalar keeps this readable, and nothing about the loss changes with dimension except that the squared error is summed over coordinates. With flow time \(u = 0.3\) and noise draw \(z = -0.85\):

\[ a^u = (1-0.3)(-0.85) + (0.3)(0.34) = -0.5950 + 0.1020 = -0.4930, \qquad a - z = 0.34 - (-0.85) = 1.1900. \]

If the network predicts \(v_\theta(a^u, u, o) = 0.95\), the loss is \((0.95 - 1.19)^2 = (-0.24)^2 = 0.0576\). That is the entire update: a squared error on a sample chosen because its advantage was high. No ratio, no clip, no old policy, no critic pass.

# --- PPO: the update needs the old policy's log-probs ---
logp_old = batch.logp                          # frozen, stored at collection time
for epoch in range(10):                        # several passes over the SAME batch
    logp  = policy.log_prob(batch.obs, batch.act)      # recomputed every epoch
    ratio = torch.exp(logp - logp_old)                 # this is rho_t
    loss  = -torch.min(ratio * batch.adv,
                       torch.clamp(ratio, 1-eps, 1+eps) * batch.adv).mean()
    loss  = loss + vf_coef * v_loss - ent_coef * entropy   # critic + exploration
batch.discard()                                # now off-policy: unusable

# --- VLA-RL: the update never asks how likely the action was ---
idx  = sample(pool, p=softmax(clip(pool.adv, -2, 2)))   # advantage enters HERE
o, a = pool.obs[idx], pool.chunk[idx]                   # a is a whole H-step chunk
u    = torch.rand(len(idx))                             # flow time in [0,1]
z    = torch.randn_like(a)                              # fresh noise, same shape
a_u  = (1-u)[:, None, None] * z + u[:, None, None] * a  # straight-line interpolant
v    = model.velocity(a_u, u, o, cond=adv_token[idx])   # advantage also conditions
loss = ((v - (a - z)) ** 2).mean()                      # unweighted: w_i lives in the sampler

Pitfalls

The characteristic error here is importing PPO reflexes into a weighted-BC pipeline, where they are not wrong so much as meaningless. There is no ratio, so tuning \(\epsilon\) or adding KL early-stopping does nothing. There is no closed-form entropy, so an entropy bonus has nothing to attach to — the diversity knob is the sampling temperature at inference. Per-minibatch advantage normalization is actively harmful when advantages come from an offline pass over a pool: use a global scale from unbiased rollouts, or a batch of pure failures will manufacture positive advantages out of nothing. And "run more environment steps" is usually the weakest lever you have.

9.4 What transfers unchanged

Now the reassuring part: your PPO experience is the vocabulary the new world is written in.

The MDP framing. States, observations, actions, transitions, episodes, discounting: identical. When LeHome sets \(\gamma = 0.999\), you know why a 600-frame horizon demands a number that close to one.

Returns, values, advantages. \(G_t\), \(V^\pi(s)\), \(Q^\pi(s,a)\) and \(A^\pi = Q^\pi - V^\pi\) mean exactly what they meant in Chapter 1. Chapter 8's future-prediction head is "Q-like" because it conditions on the actions being generated; the success head is "V-like" because it does not. And "how much better than the policy's own average was this?" is the same question in both worlds. Only the consumer changes: a multiplier on a log-probability gradient in one, a sampling probability and a conditioning token in the other.

GAE mechanics and baselines. The backwards recursion over \(\delta_t\), the bias-variance dial on \(\lambda\), the bootstrap at episode boundaries — unchanged, right down to the code; the loop just runs offline over a pool instead of inline over a batch. And Chapter 2's proof that subtracting any state-dependent baseline leaves the gradient unbiased while shrinking its variance is exactly what licenses the damped baseline \(\alpha(\hat{p}_{succ} - R_{acc})\) and the group anchor of Chapter 11.

Curve-reading. Watching for value-loss explosions, distrusting a success curve that climbs while the value head saturates, noticing a run that has collapsed to one behavior — these instincts transfer completely, and matter more here, because in an asynchronous pipeline a bad checkpoint keeps poisoning the pool long after a lockstep loop would have shown you the problem.

9.5 What does not transfer

And the honest list of what to put down.

The ratio, the clipped objective, and per-step log-probabilities. Gone, for the reason worked through in 9.3(b), and with them every method whose derivation needs \(\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\): vanilla policy gradients, PPO, GRPO, most maximum-entropy RL (the family, SAC among them, that adds an entropy term to the reward itself). If a paper here mentions clipping, look twice — it is almost certainly clipping the advantage into a range like \([-2, 2]\) before exponentiating it, which is a different operation entirely.

The entropy bonus as the exploration knob. To get more diverse behavior from a flow policy you raise the noise temperature, lower the guidance scale \(g\), or add demonstrations of the behavior you want. You do not add a term to the loss.

Lockstep batches, and the instinct that environment steps are the main lever. Three checkpoints are in flight at once, and every episode carries value predictions from whichever one collected it. In PPO more steps almost always helps; here the marginal 500 rollouts are usually worth less than fixing per-source sampling shares, adding 50 targeted DAgger corrections, or re-tuning execution length. That is the hardest habit to unlearn, because it was correct for years.

On your rig

Your three projects sit at three different points on this comparison, which is a lucky position to be in. rl-rover is the pure left-hand column: small action space, cheap simulator, Gaussian head, lockstep PPO, entropy bonus and all. hil-serl on the SO-101 is the middle ground of 9.6: off-policy actor-critic with a small Gaussian head, so log-probs exist and Q-learning is on the table, but data lives in a buffer and a human supplies exploration — your 98 percent intervention run is that dial turned all the way up. A LeRobot flow-matching policy on the same arm would be the right-hand column: same hardware, entirely different training loop.

9.6 When to use which

Neither family is universally better. The decision is usually made for you by two facts: what a sample costs, and whether you have a prior worth keeping. Figure 9.3 puts the questions in order.

Reach for the PPO family when the spaces are small, the simulator is fast, there is no pre-trained prior to preserve, and you need the policy to discover behavior nobody has demonstrated — locomotion gaits, a rover learning to drive, game agents. PPO's exploration is real exploration.

Reach for the weighted-BC and conditioning family when samples are expensive, demonstrations exist or can be produced, the action distribution is genuinely multimodal, and you start from a pre-trained VLA whose broad competence you must not destroy. Real-world manipulation is almost always in this box. What you accept: limited exploration, in exchange for stability and the use of every episode you have ever collected.

The middle ground is real and you already run it. HIL-SERL keeps a small Gaussian head, so log-probabilities still exist and off-policy actor-critic and Q-learning remain available — and Q-learning genuinely propagates value backwards through a buffer in a way weighted BC does not. What it gives up is the multimodal chunk generator and the pre-trained visual-language prior; exploration comes from a human on an Xbox pad. For one task on one arm it is often the fastest path to a working policy (Chapter 13).

9.7 One task, seen through both lenses

Make it concrete. The task: fold a towel lying flat on a table with a bimanual arm setup, judged by a geometric check on keypoint positions at the end of the episode.

9.7.1 The PPO attempt

You would write a Gymnasium environment around a physics simulator: a 12-dimensional joint-velocity action space, a Gaussian over those 12 numbers at 30 Hz, 2048 steps per batch, four epochs, \(\epsilon = 0.2\). You would fight the reward first, because binary success at the end of a 600-step episode is a needle in a haystack, so you shape it into per-step terms for corner proximity, flatness and grasp contact.

Then it gets stuck, in three ways. Exploration: random joint-space noise essentially never grasps a cloth corner, so your shaping terms are the only thing keeping the run alive, and the policy finds whatever degenerate behavior maximizes them — dragging the towel into a heap that scores well on flatness is the classic. Multimodality: folding left-first and right-first are both correct, and a Gaussian averages them into a motion that grabs neither. Sample cost: a deformable-cloth step is not microseconds, and PPO's appetite runs to tens of millions of them.

9.7.2 The VLA-RL attempt

You start from a pre-trained VLA for which grasping a cloth corner is behavior it can already produce — unreliably, but it produces it — and fine-tune on a few hundred demonstrations. Now the flywheel: workers collect episodes, most failing; a success head trained on those binary outcomes gives \(\hat{p}_{succ}\) and hence a value; a completion head gives shaping; GAE over the pool gives per-frame advantages. Training draws frames with probability proportional to \(\exp(\hat{A}_i/\beta)\), so good episodes dominate the gradient. Multimodality is a non-issue: the flow model represents both fold orders, and best-of-\(N\) picks between them per chunk.

Then this gets stuck, also in three ways. Exploration: if the policy never once succeeds on a garment type, the advantage signal is flat and there is nothing to re-weight, so you must go collect demonstrations — the algorithm will not invent the behavior. Staleness: predictions attached to two-week-old episodes came from a much worse checkpoint, and unless you decay or re-anchor them they drag the advantage estimate. Local optima: the fence closes around a mediocre strategy, and LeHome's answer was to roll back to an earlier checkpoint and re-diversify, three times in the online round alone.

In the LeHome solution

Every row of the right-hand column is visible in the winning run (Larchenko, 2026). No log-probability appears anywhere: the advantage enters through the sampler as \(P(i) \propto e^{\operatorname{clip}(\hat{A}_i,\,-2,\,2)}\) — the same \(w_i\) at \(\beta = 1\), moved off the loss — leaving the flow-matching loss a plain unweighted MSE, plus a RECAP-style advantage token conditioning generation. The value is the success head, \(V = \hat{p}_{succ} - R_{acc}\), damped by \(\alpha = 0.5\). Trainer, rollout workers and the DAgger station share only a Hugging Face repository. The final pool held roughly 12,500 rollout episodes and 4.3 million frames, old datasets decaying by 0.98 per iteration. Exploration lived at inference: guidance \(g\) around 7 to 9, best-of-\(N\) with \(N = 2\) or 3.
