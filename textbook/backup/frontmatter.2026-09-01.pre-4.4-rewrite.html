Preface

This book answers one question: what do you need to understand, and in what order, to train a modern learned policy on a real robot — concretely, to replicate the winning solution of the LeHome Challenge (ICRA 2026), in which a bimanual SO-ARM101 robot learned to fold garments, first in simulation and then in the real world.

The LeHome solution is the running example because it is unusually complete and unusually open: code, tech report, checkpoints, and a three-part video series explaining every choice. One project touches nearly everything that matters in robot learning today — policy gradients and their limits, offline RL as weighted behavior cloning, diffusion and flow-matching action policies, vision-language-action models, reward and advantage engineering, inference-time optimization, human-in-the-loop data collection, and sim-to-real transfer. Follow the thread of this one problem and you cover the field.

About this second edition

The first edition was correct but compressed: it stated results where it should have built them. This edition roughly triples the explanation while keeping every equation. Three things changed in particular.

Nothing is asserted before you feel the problem. Each idea now arrives in the order it was actually discovered: here is what we want; here is the obvious thing to try; here is precisely how it fails; here is the fix, and why it works. The fix alone is a fact to memorize. The sequence is understanding.

Generative policies get a real on-ramp. The first edition compressed multimodality, flow matching, VLAs, and auxiliary heads into a single chapter. They are now five: Chapter 5 explains what a generative model even is and why a Gaussian stopped being enough; Chapter 6 teaches diffusion from zero; Chapter 7 does flow matching; Chapter 8 covers vision-language-action models; and Chapter 9 puts PPO and VLA-RL side by side, component by component, so you always know which world you are standing in.

Every equation is unpacked in prose. Mathematics is the compressed form of an idea, not the idea itself. Each important equation is followed by a plain-words box that names every symbol and says what the formula does. You should never have to decode notation alone.

How to read it

Chapters build strictly on one another; read them in order. Parts I and II take you from the MDP to the single most important update rule in the book. Part III is the conceptual pivot and the longest stretch — go slowly there, especially if diffusion and transformers are new to you. Part IV is engineering, Part V is your project.

Six kinds of box recur, and each has a job:

  • In plain words — the preceding equation, restated in English, every symbol named.
  • Words you will hear — vocabulary you will meet in papers and codebases around this topic, including terms this book does not otherwise use.
  • In the LeHome solution — where the idea appears in the running example, with the real numbers.
  • On your rig — how it connects to your own rover, HIL-SERL, and SO-101 work.
  • Pitfalls — the mistakes that cost people weeks, including ones you have already hit.
  • Checkpoint — at each chapter's end, what you should now be able to explain. If a bullet does not feel true, that section is worth a second pass before moving on.

Long derivations are sketched and referenced out to the original papers. Appendix A tells you which papers to read deeply, which to read once, and which to skim. Appendix B pairs every chapter with one concrete experiment on hardware you already own — reading a chapter without running its rung is half the lesson.

What this book assumes

Engineer-level probability and calculus: you can read an expectation, a gradient, and a Gaussian without looking anything up, and you are not expecting measure theory. Working Python. Some hands-on RL: you have trained an agent before and watched its learning curves. Everything past that — and all of Part III in particular — is built here from the ground up. No prior exposure to diffusion models, transformers, or vision-language models is assumed anywhere.

This book is one half of a pair. The other half is the roadmap (ROADMAP.md) that schedules these chapters against five months of building. The book tells you how things work; the roadmap tells you when to care.

Notation

One table, used everywhere. The only notational trap in the book is the difference between the environment's timestep \(t\) and the generative model's internal time \(u\) (or step index \(k\)); Chapters 6 and 7 warn about it again where it bites.

\(s,\ o,\ a\)state, observation, action
\(t\); \(a_{t:t+H}\)environment timestep; action chunk with horizon \(H\)
\(\pi_\theta,\ \pi_\beta,\ \pi_{\theta_{old}}\)learned policy, behavior/demonstrator policy, pre-update policy
\(\tau,\ r_t,\ \gamma,\ G_t\)trajectory, reward, discount, return \(G_t=\sum_k \gamma^k r_{t+k}\)
\(V^\pi,\ Q^\pi,\ A^\pi,\ \hat{A}_t\)value, action-value, advantage \(Q^\pi{-}V^\pi\), advantage estimate
\(\delta_t,\ \lambda\)TD residual \(r_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)\); GAE parameter

\(w_i=\exp(\hat{A}_i/\beta)\)AWR weight with temperature \(\beta\)
\(k,\ \bar\alpha_k\)diffusion noise-level index and cumulative signal coefficient (Chapter 6)
