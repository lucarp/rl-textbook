Appendix B — The experiment ladder

Reading this book produces vocabulary; only experiments produce skill. This appendix turns each chapter into one concrete experiment on hardware and code you already own — the rl-boarding gridworld, the rl-rover PPO stack, the LeRobot checkout and SO-101 in hil-serl, and finally the LeHome stack itself. The rungs are ordered; each assumes the chapters and rungs before it. Times assume your 15–20 h/week and map onto the roadmap phases (table at the end).

Three rules bind every rung. One question per experiment: write it down before touching the keyboard, and design the smallest run that answers it. Measure, don't watch: every rung ends in a number or a plot, never an impression — and where runs are cheap (E2, E3) use 3–5 seeds, because single-seed RL results are noise. Log like you already do: a dated NEXT.md-style note per rung — question, setup, result, surprise. Those notes become the tech notes of roadmap Phase 5 almost verbatim.

B.1 Foundations rungs (Chapters 1–3)

E1 · Values by hand (Chapter 1; rl-boarding; ~3 h). Question: do empirical returns really converge to a value function obeying the Bellman recursion? Run the gridworld with a fixed random policy for a few thousand episodes; for two chosen cells, average the discounted returns observed from them to estimate \(V(s)\), then verify \(V(s) \approx \mathbb{E}[r + \gamma V(s')]\) using the empirical successor frequencies. Repeat at \(\gamma = 0.9\) and \(0.99\). You understood it if you can predict, before rerunning, which cells' values move most when \(\gamma\) changes — and why the far-from-goal cells feel the discount hardest.

E2 · REINFORCE from scratch, and the variance cure (Chapter 2; CartPole, already installed in the rover venv via gymnasium[classic-control]; ~5 h). Write REINFORCE in ~60 lines (softmax policy, the estimator of Section 2.1 — no library). Train 5 seeds without a baseline, then 5 with a learned value baseline. Plot the ten learning curves together and, per batch, the standard deviation of the gradient estimate. You understood it if the baseline curves are visibly tighter and you can point at the exact line where \(G_t\) became \(G_t - b(s_t)\) — and explain why that cannot change the expectation.

E3 · PPO forensics on the rover (Chapter 3; rl-rover; ~5 h). Your train_rover.py runs are the corpus. First, read a healthy run's TensorBoard against Section 3.5: approx_kl, clip_fraction, entropy_loss, value_loss. Then break it twice on short (300k-step) runs: set clip_range=10 and watch for the collapse Section 3.1 predicts; set gae_lambda=0.0 and watch early learning starve while the critic is still bad. Finally, dump one episode's rewards and policy.predict_values() outputs and recompute \(\hat{A}_t\) by hand at \(\lambda \in \{0, 0.95\}\), reproducing the Section 3.5 arithmetic on your own data. You understood it if you can diagnose, from curves alone, which knob a stranger's broken PPO run needs.

B.2 The pivot rungs (Chapters 4–6)

E4 · AWR on your own logged data (Chapter 4; rl-rover; ~7 h). Build an offline dataset: roll out a mediocre checkpoint (an early ppo_rover_v0*.zip) for ~500 episodes, storing observations, actions, and returns. Train two fresh policies by supervised regression on the actions: one unweighted (BC), one weighted by \(w_i = \exp(\hat{A}_i/\beta)\) with \(\hat{A}_i = G_i - V(s_i)\) from the checkpoint's own critic, advantages normalized, weights clipped at 20. Evaluate both on 100 episodes. You understood it if weighted beats unweighted, and you can show the histogram of weights and say what \(\beta\) is doing to it — then push \(\beta\) toward 0 and watch the dead-data failure of the pitfall box appear.

E5 · Train a generative policy and see multimodality (Chapter 5; LeRobot in hil-serl, GPU; ~8 h). Train LeRobot's diffusion policy (or the flow-matching pi0/pi05 ports your checkout already ships) on an existing SO-101 dataset — your 24-episode cube set works. Then interrogate it: freeze one observation, sample 50 action chunks, and plot the first action dimension's spread — the mode structure of Figure 5.1 made visible on your own data. Find which config fields play \(H\), \(K\), and the execution length: \(H\) is chunk_size in the pi0-family and horizon in diffusion; the execution length is n_action_steps in both. You understood it if you can explain why this policy has no usable log_prob method while E2's sixty-liner did.

E6 · Minimal RECAP: one conditioning bit (Chapter 6; hil-serl data, GPU; ~8 h). Conditioning needs failures, and your cube dataset is 24/24 successes — so mix in failure episodes from your HIL-SERL run buffers, or record ~10 deliberate near-misses. Label every episode's chunks \(c \in \{+,-\}\) by outcome, append the bit as one extra policy input, train with ~30% condition dropout, and evaluate the policy queried at \(c{=}+\) against an unconditioned twin trained on the same mixed data. You understood it if \(c{=}+\) recovers (or beats) the clean-data policy while the unconditioned twin is dragged down by the failures — conditioning turned bad data into signal instead of poison.

B.3 Engineering rungs (Chapters 7–8)

E7 · Shaping honestly, and CUPED on your logs (Chapter 7; rl-rover; ~6 h). Two parts. (a) Add a deliberately broken progress bonus to the rover — raw “+k when distance shrinks”, not potential-based — and short-train until you catch the policy farming it (oscillating toward/away from the goal); then write the same idea as \(\gamma\Phi(s')-\Phi(s)\) and confirm the exploit dies. (b) From E3's logged episodes, compute \(\rho_{GV}\) between returns and the critic's predictions, then \(\alpha^* = \rho_{GV}\,\sigma_G/\sigma_V\), and verify numerically that \(\mathrm{Var}(G-\alpha^* V) < \mathrm{Var}(G - V)\) whenever the critic is noisy. You understood it if you can state which of your two baselines (E3's critic early vs. late in training) deserves \(\alpha\) closer to 1, before computing it.

E8 · Inference knobs and a bandit to tune them (Chapter 8; E5's policy in sim or on the arm; ~5 h). The checkpoint is frozen; only execution changes. Sweep n_action_steps over {1, 8, half of \(H\), \(H\)} at ~30 scored episodes each, and keep the curve. Then hand the same choice to the Beta–Thompson bandit of Section 8.5 (the ~15-line implementation) and let it allocate the same episode budget online. Skip best-of-N for now — you have no Q-like head until the capstone. You understood it if the bandit's final posteriors rank the arms the way your sweep did, while having spent most of its episodes on the contenders instead of the losers.

B.4 Human-and-real-world rungs (Chapters 9–10)

E9 · Close the HIL-SERL loop, and watch the human retire (Chapter 9; hil-serl, the run NEXT.md says is one USB cable away; 2–3 robot sessions). Finish the cube run to a clean success rate over ≥50 scored episodes. Instrument what Chapter 9 predicts: log the intervention flag per frame and plot intervention rate per 10-episode window across the sessions — it should decay as the policy absorbs the corrections. Keep the neutral-action histogram from your RB-release bug as a permanent dashboard check. Then implement Section 9.4's weighting in one DAgger-style refit: corrections up-weighted, pre-takeover ramp to zero. You understood it if you can show the decay curve and say what a flat one would have told you.

E10 · Async at N=2 (Chapter 10; rover-mujoco; ~9 h). Split rover training into Figure 10.1's roles on one machine: a learner process consuming episode files, plus rollout workers that load the newest checkpoint from a shared directory (a filesystem stands in for the Hub), each running the MuJoCo rover. Measure episodes/hour at 1 vs. 2 workers, then kill a worker mid-run and confirm nothing but throughput suffers. Use AWR-style weighted updates on the learner — then note, per Section 10.4, why the same architecture with PPO would be quietly wrong as the checkpoints lag. Your existing audit_baseline.py solvability check is the companion piece: rerun it whenever you widen randomization. You understood it if the worker crash cost you throughput and nothing else, and you can say which single property of the update rule made that true.

B.5 The capstone gate (Chapter 11)

E11 · Stage A, exactly as written (Chapter 11; LeHome repos, GPU; 1–2 weeks). Clone the challenge environment and the solution repo, get Isaac Lab rendering, run his sim checkpoint through the official evaluator on the public garments, and land within a few points of the published per-type numbers (74.5 / 70.0 / 80.5 / 93.5). Write the one-page codebase map Section 11.2 demands. This rung is deliberately unoriginal: it converts the whole book from prose into a running system you have verified, and every later capstone stage stands on it. You understood it if your reproduced score has a confidence interval attached (Section 11.1) and you can point, in his code, at where each auxiliary head attaches and where the sampler applies the AWR priorities.

Pitfalls
Climbing rungs without the gates. The temptation is to “run” E4 or E6, see a plausible curve, and move on. The gate is the stated measurement plus the you understood it if test, spoken aloud or written — an experiment that produced a number you cannot explain has not been finished, it has been visited.
