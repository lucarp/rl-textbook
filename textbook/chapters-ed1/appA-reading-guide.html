Appendix A — Paper-by-paper reading guide

This appendix is the book's bibliography turned into a study plan: one compact entry per paper, grouped to mirror the chapters. Each entry carries a tier. Tier 1: read deeply, pen in hand — work the derivations the book only sketched, and reproduce at least one figure or equation yourself. Tier 2: read once, for the single idea the book borrows; do not fight the proofs. Tier 3: skim, or keep on file as a reference you return to when a detail matters. Read in book order, not chronological order: the chronological path wanders through a decade of dead ends, while the book order takes you from policy gradients to LeHome along the shortest line. Chapter numbers below tell you when each paper becomes readable.

A.1 Foundations and policy gradients (Chapters 1–2)

REINFORCE (Williams, 1992) — Machine Learning 8(3–4). Tier 2. The original score-function gradient estimator and the first proof that subtracting a baseline leaves the gradient unbiased. Read Sections 1–2 and the algorithm statement; skip the episodic variants and the 1992-era network machinery. Chapter 2 rederives everything you actually need.

Policy gradient theorem (Sutton, McAllester, Singh, and Mansour, 1999) — NeurIPS 1999. Tier 2. Shows the gradient of \(J(\theta)\) needs no derivative of the state distribution, which is why policy gradients are computable at all. Read the theorem and the function-approximation section; skim the convergence result.

A.2 Trust regions, PPO, and GAE (Chapter 3)

TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015) — arXiv 1502.05477. Tier 3. Read Sections 1–4 for the monotonic-improvement bound and the KL-constraint idea; skip the conjugate-gradient implementation entirely — PPO replaced it in practice, and the bound is what survives.

PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) — arXiv 1707.06347. Tier 1. The clipped surrogate objective you have already trained with. The paper is short; read all of it and hand-trace the clip logic for one ratio above and one below \(1 \pm \epsilon\). Reading it with your rl-rover runs open closes the loop between the equation and your TensorBoard curves.

GAE (Schulman et al., 2016) — arXiv 1506.02438. Tier 1. The \(\lambda\)-weighted sum of TD residuals \(\delta_t\) as a bias–variance dial for \(\hat{A}_t\) — used verbatim in LeHome. Read Sections 1–3 with a pen; skim the trust-region value-fitting section.

A.3 RL as weighted behavior cloning (Chapter 4)

AWR (Peng et al., 2019) — arXiv 1910.00177. Tier 1. The hinge of this book: RL as supervised regression with weights \(w_i = \exp(\hat{A}_i/\beta)\). Work through the constrained-policy-search derivation in Section 3 and the appendix — it is the one derivation everything after Chapter 4 stands on.

AWAC (Nair et al., 2020) — arXiv 2006.09359. Tier 2. AWR with an off-policy Q-function critic, built for offline pretraining followed by online fine-tuning — the shape of every modern robot pipeline. Read the introduction and Algorithm 1; skip the benchmark tables.

A.4 Generative action policies and VLAs (Chapter 5)

Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) — arXiv 2210.02747. Tier 2. Trains a velocity field \(v_\theta(a^u, u, o)\) by regression along straight-line interpolants — no likelihoods, no ODE solves during training. Read Sections 1–3 for the conditional flow-matching trick and the optimal-transport-path part of Section 4 for the straight-line interpolant; skip the general Gaussian-path derivations and the appendix proofs. Chapter 5's version suffices for practice.

Classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans, 2022) — arXiv 2207.12598. Tier 2. Train conditional and unconditional models jointly by condition dropout, then extrapolate between them with scale \(g\) at sampling time. A short workshop paper; read the whole thing. Chapter 8 reuses it on advantage conditioning.

Pi0.5 (Physical Intelligence, 2025a) — arXiv 2504.16054. Tier 1. The vision-language-action model underneath the LeHome solution: a VLM backbone with a flow-matching action expert emitting chunks \(a_{t:t+H}\). Read the architecture and co-training sections closely; skim the open-world evaluation.

A.5 Learning by conditioning (Chapter 6)

Pi*0.6 / RECAP (Physical Intelligence, 2025b) — arXiv 2511.14759. Tier 1. Advantage-conditioned policies: instead of reweighting data, feed \(\hat{A}_t\) to the policy as an input and ask for high advantage at inference. Read the RECAP method section twice — it is Chapter 6 in primary-source form — plus the value-learning details; skim the fleet results.

A.6 Reward and advantage engineering (Chapter 7)

Potential-based shaping (Ng, Harada, and Russell, 1999) — ICML 1999. Tier 2. Shaping rewards of the form \(\gamma \Phi(s_{t+1}) - \Phi(s_t)\) are the only ones guaranteed to preserve the optimal policy. Read the theorem statement and the bicycle example; skip the proofs.

CUPED (Deng et al., 2013) — WSDM 2013. Tier 3. Control variates for A/B testing; LeHome's damped baseline \(\alpha V\) is this estimator transplanted into advantage estimation. Read Section 3 for the variance-optimal coefficient; the rest is web experimentation.

GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) — arXiv 2402.03300. Tier 3. The DeepSeekMath paper. Only the group-relative advantage — scoring each rollout against the mean of its siblings — matters here; it is the anchor Chapter 7 uses against stale value heads. Read Section 4; skip all the math-benchmark content.

A.7 Inference-time optimization (Chapter 8)

Thompson sampling (Thompson, 1933) — Biometrika 25. Tier 3, a historical pleasure worth one evening. Pair it with the tutorial (Russo et al., 2018) — arXiv 1707.02038, Tier 2: read Chapters 1–4 on Bernoulli bandits with Beta posteriors — exactly the machinery that tunes LeHome's seven inference knobs online; skip the approximate-inference chapters.

Real-Time Chunking (Black et al., 2025) — arXiv 2506.07339. Tier 2. Frames chunk switching as inpainting: freeze the overlap with the executing chunk, generate the rest consistently. Read the method section; skim the latency analysis. This is the origin of the inpainting knob in Chapter 8.

A.8 Imitation and the human in the loop (Chapter 9)

DAgger (Ross, Gordon, and Bagnell, 2011) — arXiv 1011.0686. Tier 1. Why behavior cloning compounds errors quadratically in horizon and how on-policy relabeling makes it linear. Read the full paper, including the reduction argument; the no-regret machinery can be skimmed on a first pass.

HG-DAgger (Kelly et al., 2019) — arXiv 1810.02890. Tier 2. Human-gated DAgger: the expert decides when to seize control, so every intervention marks a state the policy handled badly. This is literally your RB-to-intervene gesture. Read the gating section and the risk metric; skip the driving-simulator specifics.

HIL-SERL (Luo et al., 2024) — arXiv 2410.21845. Tier 1. The pipeline you run on the SO-101. Read the full system section against your own actor/learner logs — intervention handling, reward interface, replay design — and the ablations on human corrections. Nothing to skip; it is all operational.

A.9 Simulation and rollout collection (Chapter 10)

Difficulty-targeted selection (Sun et al., 2025) — arXiv 2506.05316. Tier 3. Training signal peaks on examples the policy succeeds at roughly half the time; LeHome's hard mining applies this to rollout selection. Read the introduction and the selection rule; the LLM fine-tuning details do not transfer.

A.10 The capstone sources (Chapter 11)

LeHome tech report (Larchenko, 2026) — arXiv 2606.27163. Tier 1. The case study this book is built around: Pi0.5 plus AWR plus RECAP, auxiliary heads, the full advantage formula, inference-time bandits, and the sim-to-real playbook. Read it twice — once after Chapter 6, when the training story will parse, and again line by line before starting the capstone.

BEHAVIOR Challenge report (Larchenko et al., 2025) — arXiv 2512.06951. Tier 2. The earlier win that seeded LeHome's architecture choices and its chunk post-processing logic. Read for lineage after the LeHome report; skim the benchmark-specific engineering.

A.11 Beyond papers

Sutton and Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, 2nd edition (2018). Reference. When a foundation wobbles, Chapters 3–6 (MDPs, dynamic programming, TD learning) and Chapter 13 (policy gradients) are the canonical patch. Do not read it cover to cover on this timeline.

CS224R (Chelsea Finn, Stanford). Lecture-format coverage of deep RL for robotics: offline RL, imitation, and real-robot case studies. Use the lecture on offline RL as a second angle on Chapters 4 and 6 whenever the papers feel too compressed.

LeRobot (Hugging Face). The codebase your hil-serl project already runs. Treat it as a primary source: when a paper's description of intervention handling or chunk execution is ambiguous, the resolution is in this repository, not in another paper.

Pitfalls
Reading papers without running code. A week of passive reading produces the feeling of understanding; twenty minutes of predicting what a change does to your own runs — then checking — produces the real thing. Pair every Tier 1 paper with an experiment on rl-rover or the SO-101: change \(\lambda\), change \(\beta\), break the baseline on purpose. If you cannot name the experiment a paper suggests, you have not finished reading it.

A.12 A twelve-week cadence

Weight the list so it fits the roadmap: count a Tier 1 paper as one full paper-equivalent, a Tier 2 as one half, a Tier 3 as one quarter. The list above has 8 Tier 1, 10 Tier 2, and 5 Tier 3 entries, so \(8 + 10 \times 0.5 + 5 \times 0.25 = 14.25\) paper-equivalents — just under five per month across three months at your 15–20 hours per week, leaving most of each week for the practical work. Concretely: weeks 1–4, everything in A.1–A.3 (about 4.75 equivalents) while you rebuild advantage estimation on rl-rover; weeks 5–8, sections A.4–A.7 and the Sun et al. skim (about 5.5) while the capstone's simulation stage trains; weeks 9–12, sections A.8 and A.10 (about 4; the Sun et al. skim is already behind you) while you fine-tune on the real SO-101 — ending with the second, line-by-line pass over the LeHome report exactly when you need it most.
