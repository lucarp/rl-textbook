Chapter 9 — Imitation and the human in the loop

9.1 Behavior cloning and compounding error

Chapters 4 through 8 assumed you already had a decent policy to improve. This chapter is about where that policy comes from — demonstrations — and about the cheapest, highest-leverage resource in robot learning: a human watching the robot and stepping in at the right moment.

Behavior cloning (BC) you already know from Chapter 4: supervised learning on demonstrated observation–action pairs — AWR's weighted regression with every weight set to one. No rewards, no rollouts, no credit assignment — and every VLA in Chapter 5 is BC at scale. It works remarkably well, until the robot has to run on its own for more than a few seconds.

The failure mode is covariate shift. Supervised learning guarantees low error on the training distribution: the states \( \pi_\beta \) visits. At deployment, though, the policy generates its own states. One small error steers into a state slightly unlike any demo; there the policy is a little less accurate, so the next error is larger, which produces an even stranger state. The loop feeds itself. If the per-step mistake rate on the demo distribution is \( \epsilon \) (here the supervised error rate, standard in the imitation literature — not the PPO clip parameter of Chapter 3), the classic analysis (Ross and Bagnell, 2010) shows expected cost \( J \) over an episode of length \( T \) can degrade quadratically — one mistake at step \( t \) can poison all \( T-t \) remaining steps — while a policy whose \( \epsilon \) is measured on its own state distribution pays only linearly:

\[ J\big(\pi_\theta^{\text{BC}}\big) \;\le\; J(\pi_\beta) + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\epsilon T^{2}\right), \qquad J\big(\pi_\theta^{\text{DAgger}}\big) \;\le\; J(\pi_\beta) + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\epsilon T\right). \]

Figure 9.1 is the picture to keep. Demonstrations carve a narrow tube through state space. BC is accurate inside the tube and undefined outside it — and its own small errors are exactly what carries it outside. On a real arm this looks mundane: the approach is two centimeters left of every demo you recorded, and the policy freezes or oscillates, because no demonstration ever showed what to do from there.

9.2 DAgger: label where the policy goes

The fix is not more demonstrations — it is demonstrations in the right places. DAgger (Dataset Aggregation; Ross et al., 2011) collects labels on the policy's own state distribution. Iterate: (1) roll out the current \( \pi_\theta \) (the original algorithm mixes in the expert with a decaying coefficient); (2) have the expert provide the correct action for the states actually visited; (3) aggregate those pairs with everything collected so far; (4) retrain. After a few rounds the training distribution converges toward the states the policy really induces, and the quadratic blow-up collapses to the linear, on-distribution rate above — DAgger is a no-regret algorithm. Aggregation matters: training only on the newest round lets each iteration overwrite the last, and the policy oscillates.

The price is labeling. Vanilla DAgger wants the expert's action in every visited state. For a robot that is brutal: shown a paused frame, a human cannot write down a 12-dimensional joint command, and re-teleoperating every state costs as much as demonstrating from scratch. The interactive variants below exist to spend human effort only where it pays.

Pitfalls
“More demos will fix it” — usually not. Compounding error is about where the data lives, not how much there is; 500 extra demos thicken the tube without covering the states your policy drifts into. Also: never train at full weight on the frames just before a takeover — they are the mistake; ramp them to zero (see 9.4) — and never drop old rounds when aggregating — both are quiet ways to make DAgger worse than BC.

9.3 HG-DAgger: the human as the gate

HG-DAgger (Human-Gated DAgger; Kelly et al., 2019) replaces the mixing schedule with human judgment. The policy runs autonomously while the human watches; the human seizes control only when the robot heads for trouble, drives it back to safety, and releases. Writing \( \pi_H \) for the human policy (the same demonstrator \( \pi_\beta \) of Section 9.1, now acting online):

\[ \pi_{\text{HG}}(a \mid s) \;=\; \begin{cases} \pi_H(a \mid s) & \text{human engaged} \\ \pi_\theta(a \mid s) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

Only the intervention segments — states plus human actions — enter the training set; autonomous states are never relabeled. That is a feature, not a compromise. Interventions land precisely on the current policy's failure boundary: states the policy actually reaches and handles badly. The intersection is the most information-dense imitation data you can collect. As the policy improves, interventions taper off, which gives you a live gauge of policy quality (Kelly et al. turn intervention statistics into a risk estimate) and a built-in curriculum: each round's corrections target whatever the previous round still gets wrong.

A worry that stops many people: “if I splice corrections into the data, the policy sees two behaviors — won't that create conflicting, multimodal targets?” No. The policy learns \( \pi_\theta(a \mid o) \), conditioned on the observation. Each correction is recorded in the drifted, off-distribution state where it was given — a state the original demos never covered — so at that observation there is exactly one label. Genuine multimodality means conflicting actions at the same state (two operators folding the same flat shirt differently). That is real, and Chapter 5's generative policies exist partly to model it, but gated corrections do not manufacture it.

9.4 Intervention signals as a quasi-advantage

An intervention is not only a label; it is a verdict. By not touching the controller the human implicitly scored the policy's actions as acceptable; by taking over, they scored the recent actions as bad and supplied better ones. You can fold this evaluative signal into training as per-frame sampling weights — the sampler-side AWR trick of Chapter 4, with the human's gate standing in for a critic:

\[ P(\text{sample } i) \;\propto\; \exp\!\big(\hat{A}^{\text{hum}}_i / \beta\big), \qquad \hat{A}^{\text{hum}}_i = \begin{cases} A_{\text{corr}} & i \in \text{correction segment} \\ 0 & i \text{ autonomous, far from takeovers} \\ -c\,\lambda_i & i \text{ in the pre-takeover window,} \end{cases} \]

where \( A_{\text{corr}} \gt 0 \) is a fixed constant, \( \lambda_i \) ramps from 0 to 1 across the pre-takeover window, and \( c \) is large — so the sampling weight \( \exp(-c\lambda_i/\beta) \) decays from 1 toward 0 as the takeover approaches (in practice you can ramp the weight itself, e.g. linearly). Correction frames get the largest weight: they are the closest thing to positive-advantage actions in the dataset, given exactly at the failure moments the policy needs covered. Ordinary autonomous frames get baseline weight — the human let them pass. And the window just before a takeover ramps to zero, because those actions are the failure; cloning them would faithfully reproduce the mistake the human interrupted. As in Chapter 4, applying weights in the sampler rather than the loss is identical in expectation but cheaper: zero-weight frames are never even loaded.

Worked example: weighting one DAgger round

You run 100 episodes at 10 Hz, 300 steps each: 30,000 frames. The human intervened 20 times; each intervention contributes a 50-frame correction and a 30-frame pre-takeover ramp. Buckets: corrections \( 20 \times 50 = 1{,}000 \) frames at weight 4.0; ramps \( 20 \times 30 = 600 \) frames at average weight 0.5 (linear ramp from 1 to 0); clean autonomous frames 28,400 at weight 1.0. Total sampling mass \( 4{,}000 + 300 + 28{,}400 = 32{,}700 \). In a batch of 256, corrections claim \( 256 \times 4{,}000 / 32{,}700 \approx 31 \) slots (12.2%), ramps about 2, autonomous about 223. Uniform sampling would give corrections only \( 1{,}000/30{,}000 = 3.3\% \), about 8.5 slots — the weighting oversamples them 3.7×. Want corrections to be a quarter of each batch? Solve \( 1{,}000w / (1{,}000w + 28{,}700) = 0.25 \), giving \( w \approx 9.6 \). One knob, directly interpretable.

9.5 HIL-SERL, and when to choose which loop

HIL-SERL (Luo et al., 2024) turns the same human gate into a full RL loop — the pipeline you already run. Four ingredients: a handful of teleoperated demos seeding an offline buffer; a reward classifier, a small network trained on human-labeled success and failure images, whose \( \hat{p}_{succ} \) is thresholded into the sparse reward \( r_t \); human interventions during autonomous rollouts, stored in the demo buffer as corrections exactly as in HG-DAgger; and an off-policy learner, RLPD (Ball et al., 2023) — an actor–critic in the style of SAC (soft actor-critic: an off-policy method training a Q-function alongside an entropy-regularized Gaussian actor) with a high update-to-data ratio, critic ensembling, layer normalization, and symmetric sampling:

\[ \mathcal{B} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{B}_{\text{demo+interv}} \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{B}_{\text{online}}, \qquad y \;=\; r_t + \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_{a' \sim \pi_\theta}\!\Big[ \min_i Q_i(s_{t+1}, a') \Big]. \]

Half of every batch is demos plus interventions, half is fresh online replay, so human data keeps steering the critic long after it was collected. The actor/learner split you run in project hil-serl is the point (Chapter 10 scales the same asynchronous pattern up): the actor process executes on the arm and streams transitions, the learner consumes them and ships back weights, and the human sits beside the robot with a gamepad. Interventions do double duty — imitation targets for the actor, and on-policy evidence about failure states for the critic. The reported results: near-100% success on real tasks (insertion, assembly, dual-arm coordination) within 1–2.5 hours of real-world training per task, roughly 2× the success rate and 1.8× the execution speed of imitation baselines. Early on the human intervenes constantly; by the end, almost never. The human is a hard-negative miner that smoothly retires. Figure 9.2 shows the loop.

On your rig
Your gate is RB on the pad; Y/B label success/failure for the reward classifier. You have already met the silent failure: release RB a moment early and the pipeline logs neutral (zero) actions as if someone chose them — poisoning both buffers. The general lesson: instrument the data path. Log the intervention flag per frame, histogram actions per source, alert on spikes at neutral. Every HIL pipeline has one of these bugs; the good ones make it visible.

So when do you choose HIL-RL, and when DAgger on a big pretrained policy? HIL-SERL trains a small, task-specific policy from scratch: it wants a rig you can occupy for hours, cheap resets, and a policy class with tractable log-probabilities — which, per Chapter 5, a flow-matching VLA denies you. DAgger-on-a-VLA starts from a strong generalist prior and spends each human minute refining it, with plain weighted BC as the update and no critic to train on-robot. The LeHome real-world final is the textbook case for the latter: about one week of robot time, a sim-pretrained flow VLA, and no access to the evaluation rig. Larchenko (2026) made human-gated DAgger the main real-world tactic, weighted along the lines of Section 9.4 (plus downweighted distant autonomous frames) — and Chapter 11's capstone will have you make the same choice deliberately on your SO-101.

In the LeHome solution
DAgger was the backbone of the real-round week: 471 DAgger episodes against 321 plain teleop, on a bimanual rig where the leader arms continuously track the followers so a foot-pedal takeover is jump-free. Per-frame quasi-advantage weights: corrections highest, distant autonomous frames low, the five seconds before each takeover ramped to zero. In sim, DAgger underperformed — the policy outgrew the operator — so the flywheel instead minimized human idle time: parallel sims and queued hard states keep the operator correcting, never waiting.
