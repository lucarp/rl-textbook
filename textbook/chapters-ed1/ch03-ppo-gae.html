Chapter 3 — Trust regions, PPO, and advantage estimation

Chapter 2 left you with an estimator: push up the log-probability of actions in proportion to their advantage. It did not tell you how far to push. This chapter is about that question — why step size dominates everything else in policy optimization, how TRPO answered it with a constraint, how PPO answered it with a clip, and how GAE turns noisy rewards into usable advantage estimates. PPO plus GAE is the workhorse you already ran on the rover, and even though PPO itself will not survive the jump to flow-matching VLAs in Chapter 5, the advantage machinery built here carries through the entire book.

3.1 Why step size is the whole game

In supervised learning, an overly aggressive gradient step is cheap: the dataset does not care, and the next step on the same data pulls you back. In on-policy RL the data is the policy. A destructive update produces a worse policy, which then collects worse data, which produces worse gradients. There is no fixed dataset to return to; the distribution of states you train on moves with every update. One bad step can collapse a policy that took millions of steps to build, and the collapse is often unrecoverable because the informative states — the ones near success — are no longer visited at all. Step size is not a tuning detail. It is the central difficulty.

The vanilla policy gradient makes this worse by being wasteful: the estimator is only valid at the parameters that collected the data, so in principle you get one gradient step per batch, then must throw the batch away. To reuse a batch for several updates we need importance sampling: reweight each sample by how much more (or less) likely the new policy is to have produced it. Define the importance ratio

\[ \rho_t(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)}, \]

where \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\) is the policy that collected the data. The surrogate objective

\[ L(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\, \rho_t(\theta)\, \hat{A}_t \,\right] \]

has the same gradient as the true objective \(J(\theta)\) at \(\theta = \theta_{old}\), so ascending it is locally correct. But it is only a local approximation: it reweights actions while pretending the state distribution has not moved. The farther \(\rho_t\) drifts from 1, the bigger the lie. Everything in the next two sections is a strategy for ascending this surrogate while keeping the lie small.

3.2 TRPO in one paragraph

Trust Region Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2015) makes the "stay close" requirement explicit: maximize the surrogate \(L(\theta)\) subject to a constraint that the average KL divergence — a measure of how different two distributions are — between \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\) and \(\pi_\theta\) stays below a small threshold. The theory is clean: within the trust region, improving the surrogate provably improves (a lower bound on) the true objective, so each update is monotonic in a well-defined sense. The implementation is not clean: enforcing the constraint requires second-order machinery — a Fisher-vector-product approximation of the KL curvature, conjugate gradient to solve for the step direction, then a backtracking line search. It is heavy, hard to combine with shared convolutional or recurrent architectures, and awkward to scale. TRPO's lasting contribution is the framing; the practical tool came two years later.

3.3 PPO: clip the surrogate

Proximal Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2017) gets the effect of a trust region without the second-order machinery. Instead of constraining the KL, it modifies the objective so that pushing \(\rho_t\) beyond a small interval around 1 earns nothing:

\[ L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\, \min\!\Big( \rho_t(\theta)\, \hat{A}_t,\;\; \mathrm{clip}\big(\rho_t(\theta),\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon\big)\, \hat{A}_t \Big) \right] \]

with the clip parameter \(\epsilon\) typically 0.1–0.3. Unpack the min and clip separately for the two signs of the advantage, and follow along in Figure 3.1:

  • \(\hat{A}_t \gt 0\) (the action was better than expected, so we want to raise its probability): the objective grows linearly with \(\rho_t\) but is capped at \((1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\). Once the new policy already assigns the action \(1+\epsilon\) times its old probability, the gradient vanishes — no further reward for pushing harder on this sample.
  • \(\hat{A}_t \lt 0\) (the action was worse than expected, so we want to lower its probability): the objective is flat for \(\rho_t \lt 1-\epsilon\). Once the probability has been pushed down by a factor \(1-\epsilon\), the gradient dies. But for \(\rho_t \gt 1\) the unclipped, increasingly negative branch survives: if an earlier minibatch update accidentally raised the probability of a bad action, the objective still punishes that at full strength.

That asymmetry is the point of taking the min: it makes the objective pessimistic. Improvements beyond the clip range are ignored; deteriorations are never ignored. The clip only removes the incentive to leave the trust region — it is an incentive structure, not a hard constraint, and a large learning rate can still jump past it. That is why you monitor the KL anyway (Section 3.5).

Because the clip keeps updates near \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\), PPO can afford to reuse data: collect a rollout of \(N\) steps from several parallel environments, then run several epochs of minibatch stochastic gradient descent over it before collecting fresh data. The full loss adds two terms: a value loss \((V_\phi(s_t) - \hat{G}_t)^2\) that trains the critic the advantage estimator needs, and an entropy bonus that pays the policy a small amount for staying stochastic, delaying premature convergence to a deterministic — and possibly wrong — behavior.

# One PPO iteration
batch = collect_rollout(pi_old, n_envs=8, n_steps=2048)   # 16384 steps
adv   = gae(batch.rewards, batch.values, gamma, lam)      # Section 3.4
adv   = (adv - adv.mean()) / (adv.std() + 1e-8)
for epoch in range(4):
    for mb in minibatches(batch, size=512):
        rho   = exp(logp(pi, mb.a) - mb.logp_old)
        l_pi  = -min(rho * mb.adv,
                     clip(rho, 1-eps, 1+eps) * mb.adv).mean()
        l_v   = (v(mb.s) - mb.returns).pow(2).mean()
        loss  = l_pi + 0.5 * l_v - 0.01 * entropy(pi, mb.s)
        loss.backward(); opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()
pi_old = pi

PPO became the field's default for a simple reason: it is a first-order method — a dozen lines on top of an actor-critic — that is robust across a wide range of hyperparameters and scales trivially with parallel environments. It gives up TRPO's guarantees for TRPO's results at a fraction of the complexity. Note what it requires, though: an exact \(\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\), differentiable in \(\theta\), for every sampled action. Chapter 5 shows that flow-matching policies cannot provide this, which is why the LeHome solution had to leave PPO behind.

3.4 Advantage estimation: from Monte Carlo to TD, and GAE between

The clipped objective consumes \(\hat{A}_t\), and the quality of that estimate largely determines the quality of learning. There are two pure options, and they fail in opposite directions.

Monte Carlo (MC). Use the sampled return: \(\hat{A}_t = G_t - V(s_t)\), with \(G_t = \sum_k \gamma^k r_{t+k}\). This is unbiased — it is the actual outcome — but its variance is enormous, because it sums every stochastic event between \(t\) and the end of the episode: environment noise, exploration noise, lucky and unlucky bounces, all credited to the single action \(a_t\).

Temporal-difference (TD) bootstrap. Use the one-step TD residual \(\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)\): the immediate reward plus the critic's estimate of everything after, minus the baseline. Only one step of real randomness enters, so variance is low — but the estimate inherits every error in \(V\). Early in training, when the critic is bad, \(\delta_t\) is systematically wrong: it is biased.

Between the extremes sit \(k\)-step estimators (sum \(k\) real rewards, then bootstrap), and Generalized Advantage Estimation (Schulman et al., 2016) blends all of them with exponential weights:

\[ \hat{A}_t^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} \;=\; \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l}, \qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t). \]

The GAE parameter \(\lambda \in [0,1]\) is a bias–variance dial (Figure 3.2). At \(\lambda = 0\) the sum collapses to \(\delta_t\): pure bootstrap, minimal variance, full exposure to critic error. At \(\lambda = 1\) the telescoping sum reproduces \(G_t - V(s_t)\): pure MC with a baseline, unbiased, maximal variance. In practice \(\lambda \approx 0.95\) sits near the MC end: trust real rewards for the next twenty-ish steps, let the critic absorb the far tail.

Keep \(\gamma\) and \(\lambda\) mentally separate, because they look alike in the formula and are nothing alike in meaning. \(\gamma\) is part of the problem definition: it says how much the agent should care about distant rewards, and changing it changes the optimal policy. \(\lambda\) is part of the estimator only: it trades bias against variance in \(\hat{A}_t\) and, with a perfect critic, any \(\lambda\) estimates the same quantity. Tune \(\gamma\) to the task's time horizon; tune \(\lambda\) to how much you trust your critic.

3.5 PPO in practice: the rover, the curves, and one example by hand

On a task the size of rl-rover, a sane configuration is 8 parallel environments, 2048 steps each per rollout (a 16,384-step batch), 4 epochs of minibatch updates, \(\gamma = 0.99\), \(\lambda = 0.95\), \(\epsilon = 0.2\), and a budget of roughly 5M environment steps. While it trains, four TensorBoard curves tell you nearly everything:

  • Episode reward (ep_rew_mean): the ground truth. Everything else exists to explain this curve.
  • Approximate KL and clip fraction: how far updates actually move. KL steady around 0.01–0.03 and clip fraction around 0.1–0.3 is healthy. Clip fraction near zero means the learning rate is too timid (or learning has converged); KL spiking with clip fraction above 0.4 means updates are punching through the trust region — lower the learning rate or the number of epochs.
  • Value loss: should fall, then track reward changes. A value loss that explodes when reward improves usually means return magnitudes changed and the critic is chasing them.
  • Entropy: should decay slowly. A cliff-drop in the first 10% of training means the policy went deterministic before it found anything — raise the entropy coefficient.

Worked example. A 3-step episode; \(\gamma = 0.9\); rewards \(r_0 = 0\), \(r_1 = 0\), \(r_2 = 1\) (success only at the end); critic values \(V(s_0)=0.5\), \(V(s_1)=0.6\), \(V(s_2)=0.7\), and \(V(s_3)=0\) at the terminal state. First the residuals \(\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)\):

  • \(\delta_0 = 0 + 0.9 \times 0.6 - 0.5 = 0.04\)
  • \(\delta_1 = 0 + 0.9 \times 0.7 - 0.6 = 0.03\)
  • \(\delta_2 = 1 + 0.9 \times 0 - 0.7 = 0.30\)

With \(\lambda = 0\), the advantages are just the residuals: \(\hat{A}_0 = 0.04\), \(\hat{A}_1 = 0.03\), \(\hat{A}_2 = 0.30\). The success at \(t=2\) barely reaches the first action — the estimator trusts the critic's mediocre guess at \(s_1\) instead. With \(\lambda = 0.95\), accumulate backward with factor \(\gamma\lambda = 0.855\):

  • \(\hat{A}_2 = 0.30\)
  • \(\hat{A}_1 = \delta_1 + 0.855 \times \hat{A}_2 = 0.03 + 0.2565 = 0.287\)
  • \(\hat{A}_0 = \delta_0 + 0.855 \times \hat{A}_1 = 0.04 + 0.245 = 0.285\)

The terminal success now flows back to \(t = 0\) almost undamped: 0.285 versus 0.04, a sevenfold difference in the learning signal for the first action, from the same data. (For reference, \(\lambda = 1\) gives \(\hat{A}_0 = G_0 - V(s_0) = 0.81 - 0.5 = 0.31\).) When rewards are sparse and the critic is young — the standard robot situation — high \(\lambda\) is what lets credit reach the actions that earned it.

On your rig
In Stable-Baselines3 these knobs are n_envs, n_steps, n_epochs, clip_range, gae_lambda, ent_coef; the curves are approx_kl, clip_fraction, entropy_loss, value_loss. Re-run your rl-rover training and watch clip_fraction during the reward ramp: you will see it rise exactly when the policy is changing fastest. If sim-to-real transfer got worse after a "better" run, check whether entropy collapsed early — overconfident policies randomize poorly.
Pitfalls
Two silent killers. (1) Skipping per-batch advantage normalization: raw \(\hat{A}\) magnitudes drift over training, so the effective learning rate drifts too. Normalize advantages to zero mean and unit variance per batch — but never normalize rewards mid-run with running statistics you later change, or the critic's targets shift under it. (2) Reward scaling: multiplying rewards by 100 multiplies the value loss by 10,000 and its gradients by 100. If the value loss dwarfs the policy loss, rescale rewards or the value coefficient.
In the LeHome solution
PPO never appears: a flow-matching VLA has no tractable \(\log \pi_\theta(a \mid o)\), so the ratio \(\rho_t\) cannot be computed (Chapter 5). But Section 3.4 survives intact. Larchenko's advantage pipeline uses TD residuals, Monte Carlo returns, and GAE over checkpoint rewards, with the success-probability head standing in for \(V\) — and, because that value estimate is imperfect, a damped baseline \(\alpha V\) instead of the full subtraction (Chapter 7). The estimator outlived the algorithm.
