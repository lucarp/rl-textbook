Chapter 6 — Learning by conditioning: RECAP

6.1 Tell the policy the advantage

Chapter 4 improved a policy by reweighting: clone everything, but louder where the advantage is high. The AWR weight \( w_i = \exp(\hat{A}_i/\beta) \) does exactly that — and it has a hidden cost. Data far below average gets weight near zero, so your failures are effectively discarded. Yet failures carry information: what a stalled fold looks like, which grasp slips, where the policy hesitates. Worse, weighting is invisible to the model — the policy just sees good behavior more often; it is never told which behavior was good.

The conditioning idea flips this. Instead of reweighting the data, annotate it. Train an advantage-conditioned policy \( \pi_\theta(a_{t:t+H} \mid o_t, c) \), where the extra input \(c\) encodes the advantage of the data the sample came from — a binary indicator, a small set of bins, or a learned token. Training is now plain supervised learning on all the data at equal weight: successes teach the model what \(c = {+}\) behavior looks like, failures teach it what \(c = {-}\) behavior looks like, and by contrast each sharpens the other. Nothing is wasted (Figure 6.1). The standard label is an indicator thresholded at \(\epsilon_A\) (not the PPO clip \(\epsilon\) of Chapter 3), trained jointly with an unconditioned branch:

\[ c_i \;=\; \mathbb{1}\!\left(\hat{A}_i \gt \epsilon_A\right), \qquad \min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{(o_i,\,a_i,\,c_i)\sim D}\Big[ -\log \pi_\theta(a_i \mid o_i) \;-\; \eta \, \log \pi_\theta(a_i \mid o_i, c_i) \Big] \]

with \(a_i\) a chunk \(a_{t:t+H}\), \(\hat{A}_i\) its estimated advantage, and \(\eta\) a mixing weight. At inference you fix \(c = {+}\) and sample. You are asking the model a counterfactual query: “in this state, act the way the above-threshold data acted.” Because the model has seen both conditionals, the \(c={+}\) distribution concentrates on the better modes of the data (Figure 6.2). Improvement happens at query time, not in the loss. This is sometimes called upside-down RL: turn reinforcement learning into supervised learning plus a well-chosen question.

Why does this matter so much for the generative policies of Chapter 5? Flow-matching policies have no tractable log-probability, so PPO-style gradients — which need \(\rho_t(\theta)\) and push probability away from bad actions — are unavailable, and approximating them tends to shove predictions off the narrow manifold of coherent chunks. Conditioning asks nothing of the loss: for a flow-matching policy, each \(-\log \pi_\theta\) term in the objective above stands in for the tractable surrogate you already train — the flow-matching regression of Chapter 5 — so intractability rules out ratio-based gradients, not maximum-likelihood-style surrogates. The regression target is untouched; \(c\) simply joins \(o\) as an input to \(v_\theta(a^u, u, o, c)\). The honest caveat is the same as AWR’s: conditioning redistributes probability mass within the support of the data. It selects; it does not explore. To keep improving, you re-collect rollouts with the improved policy and iterate.

6.2 Where it comes from: Pi*0.6 and RECAP

The method is the backbone of Physical Intelligence’s Pi*0.6 (Physical Intelligence, 2025b), whose recipe is called RECAP: RL with Experience and Corrections via Advantage-conditioned Policies. The model itself is the Pi0.5 architecture of Chapter 5 scaled up — a Gemma 3 4B vision-language backbone with an 860M-parameter flow-matching action expert — plus one new input: the advantage indicator, inserted literally as a text token, “Advantage: positive” or “Advantage: negative.”

The value function is a separate, smaller model: a 670M-parameter Gemma 3 VLM trained as a distributional critic, predicting a categorical distribution \( p_\phi(V \mid o_t, \ell) \) over 201 discretized value bins with a cross-entropy loss against the Monte Carlo return-to-go of each episode (no bootstrapping; \(\ell\) is the task command). Advantages derived from this critic are binarized with a per-task threshold \(\epsilon_A\) (the paper writes \(\epsilon_\ell\)), set at the 30th percentile of the critic’s outputs for that task — which keeps the fraction of negative labels roughly stable as the critic’s scale drifts. The policy is trained with the joint conditioned-plus-unconditioned objective shown above.

Three data streams feed one per-task dataset. Demonstrations are assumed good: forced \(c = {+}\). On-policy autonomous rollouts get labels computed from the critic — this is where failures enter and earn their keep. Expert teleoperated corrections — interventions during autonomous execution, exactly the RB-takeover pattern you know from HIL-SERL (Chapter 9) — are forced \(c = {+}\): the expert’s fix is presumed advantageous. The pipeline runs in stages: offline-RL pre-training of critic and policy on the multi-task demo corpus, then repeatable task-specific iterations — fine-tune critic, fine-tune policy, deploy under expert monitoring, fold new rollouts and corrections back in, repeat.

The headline numbers justify the machinery: more than double the task throughput (successful completions per hour) on the hardest laundry items and on espresso making, failure rates roughly halved, success in the 90%+ range on most tasks, a 13-hour uninterrupted autonomous espresso run, and doubled box-assembly throughput after two iterations.

6.3 Family resemblances

Return-conditioned policies. Conditioning on outcomes and then querying the good outcome is the upside-down RL and Decision Transformer lineage (Srivastava et al., 2019; Kumar et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2021), which conditioned on return-to-go \(G_t\). RECAP’s refinement is to condition on advantage instead: raw return conflates action quality with state difficulty (a mediocre action from an easy state outscores a brilliant recovery from a bad one), whereas \(\hat{A}\) is state-relative — the label means “better than typical from here.” Binarizing at a percentile additionally shrugs off critic miscalibration.

AWR versus RECAP. Weighting moves the training distribution; conditioning keeps the distribution and moves the query. They are complementary and combinable: nothing stops you from sampling data with probability proportional to \(\exp(\hat{A}_i/\beta)\) and attaching an advantage label to whatever gets sampled. Larchenko’s LeHome solution does exactly both — AWR implemented through the data sampler (Chapter 4) plus RECAP-style conditioning on top. Weighting biases what the model practices; conditioning hands you a control knob at inference.

Classifier-free guidance. Because \(c\) is just an input, you can drop it: during training, replace \(c\) with a neutral null token on a fraction of samples, so the same network learns both the conditioned and unconditioned velocity fields. At inference the two can be blended and extrapolated past the positive conditional with a guidance scale \(g \gt 1\), amplifying whatever separates high-advantage behavior from the rest — classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans, 2022), transplanted from image diffusion. Chapter 8 derives the guided velocity field and treats \(g\) as an inference-time knob; Pi*0.6 runs the same extrapolation but anchors it at the negative conditional \(v_\theta(a^u, u, o, c{=}{-})\) rather than a null token — “steering toward actions the model learned were improvements.” The prerequisite is set now, at training time: no condition dropout, no guidance later.

In the LeHome solution
Larchenko implements the conditioning not as text bins but as a single gated learned advantage token in its own attention group, plus an advantage vector injected through AdaRMS conditioning (adaptive RMS-norm — per-layer normalization modulated by a conditioning vector). Gating doubles as dropout: chunks with \(\hat{A} \lt 0\) always get the neutral token; positive chunks are masked stochastically, with neutral probability ramping from 0.5 at \(\hat{A}=0\) down to 0.1 at \(\hat{A}\ge 2\). That stochastic masking is what unlocks CFG at inference — where his bandit drove \(g\) to a startling 7–9.

6.4 Practical mechanics and a worked example

Turning this into a working pipeline takes four steps. First, score every chunk in the buffer: each stored chunk needs an \(\hat{A}_i\) from your critic — Chapter 7 builds that estimator (success-probability heads, damped baselines \(\alpha V\), GAE over shaped rewards); here we treat it as given. Recompute labels whenever the critic improves — Larchenko recomputes advantages over the whole rollout pool before every training iteration. Second, discretize: a binary indicator thresholded at a fixed percentile per task is the robust default; finer bins carry more information, but each bin sees less data and inference only ever queries the top one. Third, choose a dropout rate: somewhere between 10% and 50% of samples should train the unconditioned branch (Pi*0.6 gets this from its joint two-term loss; Larchenko from advantage-dependent masking). Fourth, audit for leakage: the label must supply information the observation does not already carry, and both labels must appear across all visually distinct slices of the data — more on this in the pitfall box.

Here is the whole machine on a toy buffer of six chunks — the same six drawn in Figure 6.1. Sort the advantages, put the threshold at the 30th percentile (between \(-0.2\) and \(+0.1\); take \(\epsilon_A = -0.05\)), and label:

A-hat:   +1.8  +0.6  +0.1  -0.2  -0.9  +0.3
sorted:  -0.9  -0.2  +0.1  +0.3  +0.6  +1.8   ->  eps_A = 30th pct = -0.05
labels:    +     +     +     -     -     +        (4 positive, 2 negative)

train:   (o1,+)->a1   (o2,+)->a2   (o3,+)->a3
         (o4,-)->a4   (o5,-)->a5   (o6,+)->a6
         + dropout copies (oi, null)->ai on ~30% of draws

test:    sample a ~ pi(. | o, c=+)      # never c=-, never null
         (unless blending with CFG     -> Chapter 8)

Note what the threshold did: chunk 3, with a barely positive \(\hat{A}=+0.1\), and even a mildly negative chunk would land on the \(+\) side — the percentile decides, not zero. All six chunks contribute gradient; the two failures teach the \(c={-}\) conditional, and at test time the query \(c={+}\) steers sampling toward the modes the four positive chunks demonstrated, as sketched in Figure 6.2.

Pitfalls
Two silent failures. Conditioning collapse: if labels correlate with something visible — one garment type mostly fails, or demos come from one camera setup — the model infers the label from pixels and ignores the token; flipping \(c\) at inference then changes nothing. Use per-task percentile thresholds and check both labels occur within every visual slice. Skipping condition dropout: without a trained unconditioned or negative branch there is nothing to contrast against, and CFG in Chapter 8 is dead on arrival.
On your rig
Your HIL-SERL setup already produces RECAP’s raw material: chunks, a binary success signal from the reward button, and RB-takeover corrections. A minimal experiment on the SO-101: label every chunk of successful episodes \(c={+}\), failures \(c={-}\), force \(c={+}\) on intervention frames, feed the label as one extra input to your BC policy with 30% dropout, and deploy conditioned on \(c={+}\). That is RECAP minus the critic; Chapter 7 upgrades the labels to real advantages.
