What §2.6 does in five beats
  1. Every estimator in Chapter 2 (REINFORCE, baseline, policy gradient theorem, actor loss) contains the same factor: \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\).
  2. For ordinary policies (softmax, Gaussian) that factor is free — a five-line closed-form computation.
  3. For modern generative robot policies (diffusion / flow matching) it is not cheaply available at all. That’s the “hidden requirement” of the title.
  4. There’s a second, arguably bigger wall: these updates push probability mass away from bad actions, which can break a policy that learned a narrow family of good motions.
  5. What survives: the advantage. The rest of the book is built on it. That’s the bridge to Part III.

1 · The thread running through every equation

Before §2.6 says anything new, point at something you already have: every method in this chapter is built from one shared ingredient. Look:

Method (section)What it computes
Policy gradient theorem (2.2) \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\Big[\sum_t A_t\; \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big]\)
REINFORCE (2.3) \(\nabla_\theta J \approx \tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} R(\tau_i)\; \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\tau_i)\)
REINFORCE with baseline (2.4) \(\nabla_\theta J \approx \tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \big(R(\tau_i)-b\big)\; \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\tau_i)\)
Actor loss (2.5) \(L = -\,\hat A\; \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\)

Four methods, one bolded factor in all of them: \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\). Here is what that thing actually is.

In plain words — the shared factor
  • \(\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\): the probability (or density) your policy assigns to action \(a\) in state \(s\). \(\theta\) = the policy’s knobs (its network weights).
  • \(\log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\): how surprised the policy was by this action. (Same information as the probability, on a log scale.)
  • \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\): for each knob, how fast that surprise would go down if you turned the knob. I.e., “the direction to turn the network so that this exact action becomes less surprising.”
  • The whole method, in English: for every action you actually took, if it turned out well, turn the knobs so that action becomes less surprising; if it turned out badly, turn them the other way.
The hidden requirement (the title of the section)
Using any of these methods means your policy can hand you an exact number for “how surprised I was by the action I just sampled” — and you can backpropagate through it. For classical policies this is so cheap that nobody thinks of it as a requirement. That invisibility is the trap: §2.6 is about the day it stops being met.

2 · When the policy is a Gaussian: the free lunch

Two common policy types. Both make the hidden requirement trivially easy.

Discrete actions: softmax log π: one lookup
The policy's output literally IS a
probability vector:  [0.7, 0.3]

sampled action a = 0  (the first one)
log pi(a|s) = log 0.7 = -0.357   # done
Continuous actions: Gaussian log π: five lines
mu, log_std = policy_net(obs)
var  = (2 * log_std).exp()
logp = -0.5 * (((a - mu) ** 2) / var
             + 2 * log_std
             + math.log(2 * math.pi)).sum(-1)
logp.backward()   # grad_theta log pi

That’s the Gaussian from the book’s code block: a bell curve over actions, with mean \(\mu\) (where the policy “aims”) and spread \(\sigma\) (how confident it is). Its log-density is plain arithmetic — no sampling, no simulation. Let’s actually do the arithmetic, one step at a time, with real numbers.

Setup — no hand-waving, all numbers. The policy is a diagonal Gaussian on a 2-D action: the rover’s left/right wheel commands. The network has output \(\mu = [0.2,\;-0.1]\) and \(\sigma = [0.5,\;0.5]\) (so \(\sigma^2 = 0.25\)). It sampled the action \(a = [0.4,\;0.1]\).
Question: what is \(\log \pi(a\mid s)\), and what is \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi\) — the thing every update actually uses?
Step 1 of 8
Read the finale like a story
  • The direction \([0.8, 0.8]\) is identical whether the action was good or bad. What flips is only the sign of the reward.
  • Good action → step \(+100 \times [0.8, 0.8]\): the mean slides toward it. “Make good actions more likely.”
  • Bad action → step \(-100 \times [0.8, 0.8]\): the mean slides away from it. “Make bad actions less likely.”
  • That second sentence — pushing away — is the seed of the next wall. The book plants it on purpose.

3 · The moment it stops being free

Now change the policy. This is what happens from Chapter 5 onward (Ch. 5: why Gaussians fail, Ch. 6: diffusion, Ch. 7: flow matching).

A modern robot policy doesn’t emit one action. It emits an action chunk \(a_{t:t+H}\): a whole block of \(H\) future actions produced in one shot. And it doesn’t compute a probability — it generates. Start from pure noise, then run the network over and over, each pass nudging the blob a little closer to “looks like real motion”:

The generation pipeline of a flow-matching (or diffusion) policy. The network \(v_\theta\) learns a velocity field or a denoiser — “nudge this toward real motion” — not a probability. There is no line in the code that outputs \(\log \pi_\theta(a_{t:t+H}\mid o)\).

So where did the probability go? The book’s sentence, written as a formula. For a continuous-time flow, the probability of the output is the probability of the input plus a running total of how much the flow stretched or squeezed space along the way (the change-of-variables formula):

\[ \log p(a) \;=\; \log p(z) \;+\; \int_0^{1} \operatorname{div} v_\theta(x_u, u)\, du \]
In plain words
  • \(\log p(z)\): you know this — \(z\) is Gaussian noise, easy.
  • \(\operatorname{div} v_\theta\): “how much is the flow spreading things out right here?” Positive = stretching, negative = squeezing.
  • The integral: walk the whole generation path, from noise to finished chunk, and keep a running total of that stretch at every point. That running total is the missing part of the log-probability.

Do you see why this is not a free lunch? To get one number — the “surprise score” for one generated chunk — you must run and backpropagate through the entire chain of denoising steps, evaluating the divergence along the way. Compare with the Gaussian: five lines of arithmetic.

Is it impossible? The book is careful here, because the strong claim — “the gradient cannot be computed at all” — is simply false, and real papers depend on that:

ApproachWhat it doesCost
Path integral (above) Recover \(\log p(a)\) by integrating the divergence of the velocity field along the generation path Unroll + backprop the full chain; numerical error; high variance
Denoising chain as its own MDP (DPPO, Flow-GRPO) Treat each denoising step as a state, each step taken as an “action”; run PPO on those steps instead A complete second RL problem nested inside the first
Verdict

In principle: computable. In practice: expensive, high-variance, nothing like the free lunch a Gaussian gives you. So in practice \(\log \pi_\theta\) is off the table — and REINFORCE, actor-critic, and PPO all stall at the same wall, for the same reason.

4 · The wall nobody warns you about: pushing away

The tractability problem above is the famous one. The book says something bolder: it is arguably not the most important obstacle. Here’s the other one.

Remember the finale of the worked example: a bad action produces a step that moves the mean away from it. A policy-gradient update does not just pull probability mass toward good actions — it also pushes mass away from bad ones. For the Gaussian in our example that’s harmless: it’s a fat, spread-out blob, and pushing mass around it just reshapes a blob. No tragedy.

A generative policy is different in exactly the way that matters. Its whole value is that it has learned a narrow family of action chunks that are physically coherent — a thin “manifold” inside the enormous space of all possible action chunks. Almost all the probability mass sits on that thin surface. And now look what “push away from bad” does to a thin surface:

Notice the asymmetry. “Pull toward good” can travel along the manifold — it just moves you to a better coherent chunk. “Push away from bad” points off the manifold, because a thin surface has no “away from bad” direction that stays on the surface. The same update formula that was harmless for the fat Gaussian is now the mechanism that produces incoherent motion.
Analogies (with their limits, as always)

Think of the policy as a painter who has learned that arm motions look like this. RL says two things: “make the good painting more likely” and “make the bad painting less likely.” The second instruction is like telling a painter never paint the sky blue — they stop producing anything that looks like a painting at all. Limit of the analogy: the real mechanism is geometric. You are literally shaving probability mass off a narrow surface, and a fat-tailed Gaussian has room to absorb that, while a narrow generative distribution does not.

5 · What survives: the advantage

So the gradient-of-the-log-probability is, in practice, off the table. Does that mean no policy improvement at all? No — and this is the turn that organizes the whole rest of the book.

Chapter 2’s central quantity is not the estimator. It is the advantage \(\hat A\): how much better than average this action (chunk) did — reward minus baseline, refined with GAE. And you can use \(\hat A\) to improve a policy without ever differentiating a log-probability:

Chapter 4
Weight the data by it — AWR

Train with weighted regression: chunks with high advantage count more, \(e^{\hat A/\beta}\). No gradient of \(\log \pi\) anywhere — it’s supervised learning with smart weights.

Chapter 10
Condition on it — RECAP

Feed \(\hat A\) to the policy as an input: “given a high score, produce a good action.” The generative model is never differentiated through a likelihood.

Chapter 11
Engineer better estimates of it

Value heads, GAE, damped baselines — everything from §2.4–2.5 about making \(\hat A\) lower-variance still applies, untouched.

Chapter 12
Rank candidates with it at inference time

Generate many candidate chunks, score each with \(\hat A\), pick the best. Policy improvement that happens at test time, not training time.

“The estimator is disposable. The advantage is not.”
That sentence is the thesis of the rest of this book.
In the LeHome solution

None of this chapter’s estimators run on Larchenko’s policy. \(\pi_{0.5}\) generates action chunks by flow matching, so \(\log \pi_\theta(a_{t:t+H}\mid o)\) — the term every equation in this chapter differentiates — is not something you can cheaply evaluate, and PPO was off the table from the first day (Larchenko, 2026). The chapter’s central quantity survives intact: his solution estimates advantages with a success-probability value head \(\hat p_{succ}\), GAE, and a damped \(\alpha V\) baseline (Ch. 11), then consumes those advantages through AWR weighting (Ch. 4) and RECAP conditioning (Ch. 10).

6 · Recap, and three questions to check on yourself

Three sentences. (1) Every policy-gradient method needs \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\) — an exact, differentiable “surprise score” for the action you sampled. (2) Gaussian policies hand you that for free; generative (diffusion/flow) policies don’t — in practice it’s off the table — and besides, these methods push mass away from bad actions, which can shred the narrow family of good motions a generative policy learned. (3) So the book changes course: keep the advantage, drop the gradient. That is the bridge to Part III.

Q1 — A colleague says: “If we can’t compute the gradient of the log-prob, we can’t learn the policy at all.” What’s true, and what’s false?

True: standard REINFORCE / actor-critic / PPO updates need that factor, so they stall. False: “can’t learn at all” — you can still improve with the advantage alone (weight data: AWR; condition on it: RECAP; rank at inference time). And “impossible” is too strong: for flows, \(\log p(a)\) is computable in principle via the path integral, and DPPO / Flow-GRPO run PPO on the denoising steps — it’s just expensive and high-variance, not unavailable.

Q2 — In the worked example, why does ∂log π/∂μ = (a − μ)/σ² point from μ toward a?

The log-density is \(-\tfrac12 (a-\mu)^2/\sigma^2 +\) const. Differentiate with respect to \(\mu\): the squared term shrinks when \(\mu\) moves toward \(a\), and the derivative works out to \(+(a-\mu)/\sigma^2\). With \(a=0.4\), \(\mu=0.2\): positive, so “increase \(\mu\)” — i.e., aim closer to the action you took. The spread \(\sigma\) is in the denominator, so a confident (narrow) policy reacts more strongly per unit of surprise.

Q3 — Why does “push away from bad” hurt a generative policy but not the Gaussian?

The Gaussian’s mass is a fat blob — pushing mass away from a bad point just reshapes the blob, and the tails absorb it. A generative policy’s mass lives on a thin manifold of physically coherent chunks (that narrowness is exactly its value). “Push away” has no direction that stays on the surface, so it shoves predictions off the manifold — into incoherent motion. Same update formula; totally different geometry.

Words you will hear (for reading papers)
  • log-likelihood / log-probability: the “surprise” number \(\log \pi(a\mid o)\); the thing every policy-gradient update differentiates.
  • likelihood: \(p(a\mid o)\) of a generated action — “how likely was that output?” For flows it’s buried in the path integral.
  • change of variables: the rule that probability of the output = probability of the input × how much the map stretched space; the continuous version accumulates that stretch (“divergence”) as an integral.
  • manifold: a thin surface in a high-dimensional space — here, the set of action chunks that look physically coherent.
  • AWR / AWAC: Advantage-Weighted Regression (Ch. 4) — policy improvement by reweighting, no \(\log\pi\) gradient.
  • RECAP: advantage-conditioned policy (Ch. 10).
  • DPPO / Flow-GRPO: methods that treat the denoising steps as their own MDP and run PPO on them.
